Complex Baseband (I/Q) & SIMD Vector Processing

In audio recording, an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) samples a single physical voltage along a wire. If you plug a microphone into a 48 kHz ADC, you get scalar numbers: $s_0, s_1, s_2 \in \mathbb{R}$.

In software-defined radio, however, every hardware receiver (RTL-SDR, HackRF, USRP, BladeRF) hands your software interleaved pairs of numbers: 

[I0, Q0, I1, Q1, I2, Q2, I3, Q3, ...]

Why do radios require complex numbers? And how do we design high-throughput systems that process these streams at gigabytes per second without cache thrashing? Let's break down the computational engineering of I/Q.

1. The Problem with Real Signals: The Negative Frequency Blindspot

Euler's formula defines a complex sinusoid as:

$$e^{j\omega t} = \cos(\omega t) + j\sin(\omega t)$$

A physical antenna captures real scalar voltages. By Euler's identity, a real cosine wave is actually the sum of two counter-rotating complex vectors:

$$\cos(\omega t) = \frac{e^{j\omega t} + e^{-j\omega t}}{2}$$

Because the Fourier transform of any real-valued signal is Hermitian symmetric ($X(-f) = X^*(f)$), the positive and negative frequency spectra are mirror images.

The Downconversion Dilemma

Suppose you tune to an amateur radio frequency (e.g., 144.390 MHz for APRS) and mix the RF carrier down to baseband using a single real local oscillator.

A transmission at +10 kHz above the carrier and an interfering transmission at -10 kHz below the carrier will both fold directly onto the exact same 10 kHz baseband audio tone! A single ADC cannot determine whether the signal was above or below the carrier.

2. Quadrature Downconversion: Creating the Analytic Signal

To resolve ambiguity, an SDR front-end splits the incoming RF signal into two paths and mixes them with two local oscillator tones shifted by 90° ($\pi/2$ radians):

  • In-Phase ($I$): Mixed with $\cos(2\pi f_c t)$
  • Quadrature ($Q$): Mixed with $-\sin(2\pi f_c t)$

By feeding both branches into dual ADCs, we obtain an analytic signal:

$$s(t) = I(t) + jQ(t) = A(t)e^{j\phi(t)}$$

Now, the vector has a direction of rotation in the complex plane. Counter-clockwise rotation indicates positive frequency $+f$; clockwise rotation indicates negative frequency $-f$.

The Nyquist Bandwidth Advantage

For real-valued ADCs, the Nyquist-Shannon theorem dictates that the maximum unaliased signal bandwidth is $f_s / 2$.

For complex baseband ($I/Q$), because the spectrum is asymmetric and does not fold over zero, the usable unaliased RF bandwidth equals the full sample rate $f_s$! A 2.0 MSPS complex stream provides a full 2.0 MHz window of radio spectrum.

3. Systems Engineering: Memory Layout & SIMD Vectorization

An RTL-SDR delivers samples as interleaved 8-bit unsigned integers (u8), centered around 127.5: 

Raw byte:   [ 128,  127,  255,  128,    0,  128, ... ]
Offset:      I=0.5, Q=-0.5, I=127.5, Q=0.5, I=-127.5, Q=0.5

Interleaved vs. Planar Layout

In C, Rust, or Zig, how you lay out this memory governs CPU cache efficiency:

Layout Pattern Memory Representation Cache & SIMD Tradeoff
Interleaved (AoS) [I0, Q0, I1, Q1, I2, Q2...] Default hardware format. Excellent for point-wise operations like magnitude $|z| = \sqrt{I^2 + Q^2}$.
Planar (SoA) [I0, I1, I2...], [Q0, Q1, Q2...] Requires deinterleaving pass. Ideal for FFTs and FIR filters where $I$ and $Q$ paths run independent convolutions.

SIMD Vectorization in Zig: The Magnitude Calculation

To detect pulses (such as Mode-S PPM pulses or CW Morse dits), we compute the instantaneous signal magnitude:

$$|z| = \sqrt{I^2 + Q^2}$$

In this codebase, Zig uses explicit 16-lane SIMD vectors (@Vector(16, f32)): 

// In SdrScout Zig DSP:
const Vec16 = @Vector(16, f32);

pub fn iq_to_magnitude_simd(i_vec: Vec16, q_vec: Vec16) Vec16 {
    const i_sq = i_vec * i_vec;
    const q_sq = q_vec * q_vec;
    return @sqrt(i_sq + q_sq);
}
Why 64 KB Chunks Fit L2 Cache

A 64 KB chunk represents 32,768 $I/Q$ sample pairs (16.384 milliseconds of real-time air at 2 MSPS).

Modern x86 and ARM cores feature 512 KB to 1 MB of dedicated L2 cache per core. A 64 KB chunk, along with its unpacked float vector workspace (~128 KB), fits entirely inside L2 cache. The CPU never stalls waiting for main memory (DRAM) during the matched filter and bit-slicing passes!

Checking Your Understanding

1. Why does an SDR sample both In-Phase (I) and Quadrature (Q) components rather than a single voltage?
  • Sampling two channels doubles hardware analog amplification power across the antenna front-end.
  • Complex representation eliminates spectral mirroring and distinguishes positive from negative frequency shifts.
  • Erlang binary pattern matching fails whenever streaming buffers contain scalar single-channel real floats.
  • Standard RF coaxial transmission cables cannot transmit electromagnetic energy without two distinct conductor lines.
Correct! A single real-valued ADC produces a symmetric spectrum where $+f$ and $-f$ fold onto each other. Quadrature mixing produces an analytic signal that preserves direction of rotation (phase) and full bandwidth.
2. If an SDR samples complex I/Q baseband at 2.4 MSPS, what is its maximum unaliased RF bandwidth?
  • Exactly 1.2 MHz because the Nyquist theorem halves sample rate for all discrete signals.
  • Exactly 4.8 MHz because combining two orthogonal channels doubles theoretical spectrum capacity.
  • Exactly 2.4 MHz because complex baseband signals eliminate conjugate negative frequency folding.
  • Exactly 0.6 MHz because anti-aliasing hardware filters discard three quarters of incoming energy.
Correct! For real sampling, bandwidth is $f_s / 2$. For complex I/Q sampling, the bandwidth spans from $-f_s / 2$ to $+f_s / 2$, yielding a total usable unaliased bandwidth of exactly $f_s$ (2.4 MHz).
