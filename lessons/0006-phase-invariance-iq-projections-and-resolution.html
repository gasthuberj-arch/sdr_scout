Phase Invariance, I/Q Projections, and the Rayleigh Limit

In Lesson 5, we saw that any radio tone detector is fundamentally a vector dot product: $\langle \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{template} \rangle$.

Now, let's stress-test that code against the physical realities of radio propagation. Two critical questions emerge:

  1. The Phase Blindspot: What happens when a radio wave arrives with an unknown phase shift? Why does a single cosine template fail, and how do $I$ and $Q$ fix it?
  2. The Rayleigh Limit: Why can FT8 pack tones just 6.25 Hz apart, while APRS requires 1000 Hz spacing?

1. The Fatal Blindspot of a Single Dot Product

In Lesson 5, our simple APRS detector took the dot product against a pure cosine wave: 

template[n] = cos(2.0 * PI * 1200.0 * n / fs)
dot = dot_product(x, template)

In the real world, a radio transmitter is miles away. The antenna distance introduces an arbitrary propagation delay $\tau$, meaning the incoming tone arrives with an unknown phase offset $\phi = 2\pi f \tau$:

$$x[n] = A \cos(\omega n + \phi)$$

Expanding using angle-addition trigonometry:

$$x[n] = A \cos\phi \cos(\omega n) - A \sin\phi \sin(\omega n)$$

When we compute the dot product against our cosine template:

$$\langle \mathbf{x}, \cos(\omega n) \rangle = A \cos\phi \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \cos^2(\omega n) \approx A \cos\phi \left( \frac{N}{2} \right)$$
The Complete Signal Dropout

Look closely at the multiplier: $\mathbf{\cos\phi}$.

What happens if the signal arrives with a phase delay of $\phi = 90^\circ$ ($\pi/2$ radians)? At 144 MHz, a $90^\circ$ phase shift corresponds to an antenna distance change of just 52 centimeters!

$$\cos(90^\circ) = 0 \implies \langle \mathbf{x}, \cos(\omega n) \rangle = \mathbf{0}$$

Even if the transmitter is outputting 100 Watts right next to your antenna, your detector outputs exactly zero! The signal is completely invisible to a single cosine template.

2. The Quadrature Solution: The I/Q Energy Detector

To make our receiver completely immune to arrival phase $\phi$, we project the incoming vector onto two orthogonal templates 90° apart:

  • In-Phase Projection ($I$): Dot product against $\cos(\omega n)$
  • Quadrature Projection ($Q$): Dot product against $-\sin(\omega n)$
$$I = \langle \mathbf{x}, \cos(\omega n) \rangle = \frac{A N}{2} \cos\phi$$ $$Q = \langle \mathbf{x}, -\sin(\omega n) \rangle = \frac{A N}{2} \sin\phi$$

Now compute the total energy (the squared magnitude $|z|^2$):

$$E = I^2 + Q^2 = \left( \frac{A N}{2} \right)^2 \cos^2\phi + \left( \frac{A N}{2} \right)^2 \sin^2\phi = \left( \frac{A N}{2} \right)^2 \underbrace{(\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi)}_{= 1}$$
Phase Invariance Achieved!

By Pythagorean identity ($\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi = 1$), the arrival phase $\phi$ cancels out completely!

Whether the wave arrives at $0^\circ, 37^\circ, 90^\circ,$ or $180^\circ$, the computed energy $I^2 + Q^2$ is completely identical. This is why complex baseband ($I + jQ$) is universal across all SDR software.

3. The Rayleigh Limit: Tone Spacing vs. Observation Time

Why can FT8 pack 8 tones separated by only 6.25 Hz, while APRS requires 1000 Hz between 1200 Hz and 2200 Hz?

This is governed by the Fourier uncertainty principle (the Rayleigh criterion):

$$\Delta f \cdot \Delta t \ge 1 \iff \Delta f_{\text{min}} = \frac{1}{\Delta t}$$

Two sinusoids at frequencies $f_1$ and $f_2 = f_1 + \Delta f$ are strictly orthogonal over a window of duration $\Delta t$ if and only if $\Delta f$ is an integer multiple of $1/\Delta t$.

Protocol Symbol Duration ($\Delta t$) Minimum Orthogonal Spacing ($1/\Delta t$) Actual Tone Spacing Used
APRS (1200 baud) $1 / 1200\text{ s} \approx 0.833\text{ ms}$ $1 / 0.000833 = \mathbf{1200\text{ Hz}}$ $1000\text{ Hz}$ (historical Bell 202 standard)
FT8 (Slow MFSK) $0.160\text{ seconds}$ $1 / 0.160 = \mathbf{6.25\text{ Hz}}$ $\mathbf{6.25\text{ Hz}}$ (exact Rayleigh bound!)
WSPR (Ultra-slow HF) $0.683\text{ seconds}$ $1 / 0.683 = \mathbf{1.46\text{ Hz}}$ $\mathbf{1.46\text{ Hz}}$ (exact Rayleigh bound!)
The Engineering Tradeoff

If you want narrow tone spacing to pack hundreds of signals into a tiny sliver of radio spectrum (like FT8), physics demands that each symbol must last a long time.

If you want fast data rates (short $\Delta t$ like APRS or packet radio), your tones must be widely separated in frequency to remain orthogonal!

4. The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) Unmasked

In FT8, there are 8 tones. Do we need to write 8 separate pairs of dot product loops?

No! That is precisely what the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) does:

$$X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] e^{-j 2\pi k n / N} = \underbrace{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \cos\left(\frac{2\pi k n}{N}\right)}_{I_k} - j \underbrace{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \sin\left(\frac{2\pi k n}{N}\right)}_{-Q_k}$$

An FFT is nothing more than a bank of $N$ simultaneous, orthogonal $I/Q$ dot products computed simultaneously using an $O(N \log N)$ divide-and-conquer algorithm!

Checking Your Mastery

1. Why does an SDR demodulator compute $I^2 + Q^2$ instead of relying on a single cosine dot product?
  • Summing two squared numbers doubles the memory throughput bandwidth across the host system bus.
  • The Pythagorean sum of orthogonal projections guarantees constant energy regardless of carrier arrival phase.
  • Erlang actor processes require binary inputs to be formatted as complex IEEE floating point pairs.
  • Hardware antennas reject electromagnetic fields whose voltage does not oscillate along two physical planes.
Correct! Because $\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi = 1$, summing the squares of the In-Phase and Quadrature dot products completely eliminates the arbitrary propagation phase delay $\phi$, preventing catastrophic signal dropouts.
2. If an amateur radio digital mode uses tones spaced by 50 Hz, what is the minimum symbol duration required to keep the tones mutually orthogonal?
  • Exactly fifty milliseconds because frequency and time scale by equal integer multiples of ten.
  • Exactly five milliseconds because high frequency tones require shorter integration windows to prevent attenuation.
  • Exactly twenty milliseconds because the Rayleigh criterion requires symbol duration equal to one divided by spacing.
  • Exactly two hundred milliseconds because real analog audio channels introduce ten times additional group delay.
Correct! By the Rayleigh criterion $\Delta t = 1 / \Delta f = 1 / 50\text{ Hz} = 0.020\text{ seconds} = 20\text{ ms}$. Any symbol shorter than 20 ms will cause spectral leakage and cross-talk between the 50 Hz tones.
