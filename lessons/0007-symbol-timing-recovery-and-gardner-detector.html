Symbol Timing Recovery, the Eye Diagram, and the Gardner Detector

Up to this point, our digital demodulators assumed a convenient fiction: that the receiver knows exactly which discrete sample corresponds to the center of each transmitted symbol.

In reality, wireless transmitters and receivers operate on independent quartz crystals. Without a dedicated physical clock line connecting them, your receiver must extract the symbol clock directly from the fluctuating data stream.

1. The Clock Drift Dilemma

Suppose an APRS transmitter sends packets at a nominal 1200 baud. Because its microcontroller crystal has slight manufacturing error and thermal drift, it actually transmits at:

$$R_{\text{tx}} = 1200.05\text{ baud}$$

Your SDR receiver samples at a nominal 48,000 Hz, but its oscillator runs at:

$$f_s = 47,998\text{ Hz}$$

The true ratio of samples per symbol is:

$$N_{\text{sps}} = \frac{47,998}{1200.05} = 39.99666\dots \text{ samples/symbol}$$
The Inevitable Eye Closure

If your software simply reads every 40th sample:

$$\text{Clock Drift per Symbol} = 40.0 - 39.99666 = 0.00334\text{ samples/symbol}$$

After transmitting a standard 100-byte packet (800 bits):

$$\Delta t_{\text{accumulated}} = 800 \times 0.00334 = 2.67\text{ samples}$$

In high-speed modes (like 9600 baud GFSK or DMR where $N_{\text{sps}} = 4$ or $5$), drifting by 2.5 samples means you are no longer sampling at the peak of the pulse—you are sampling right in the middle of the transition edge, where noise causes a 50% bit error rate!

2. The Eye Diagram: Finding the Optimal Aperture

If you slice a continuous stream of demodulated symbols into single-symbol intervals and overlay them on an oscilloscope, you produce an Eye Diagram:

Reading the Eye
  • Aperture Center ("Wide Open Eye"): The instant of maximum signal separation and maximum Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR). This is where our software must sample.
  • Aperture Width: The timing margin before intersymbol interference (ISI) corrupts the decision.
  • Cross-Over Nodes ("Closed Eye"): The zero-crossing transition boundaries between consecutive bits. Sampling here is completely ambiguous.

3. The Gardner Timing Error Detector (TED)

How can software dynamically track the center of the eye without knowing the message beforehand?

In 1986, Floyd Gardner published the definitive algorithm used in modern SDRs: the Gardner Timing Error Detector. It requires only 2 samples per symbol:

  • $x[k]$: The current on-time symbol sample.
  • $x[k - 1]$: The previous on-time symbol sample.
  • $x[k - 1/2]$: The mid-point sample halfway between them.

The Gardner error formula is remarkably compact:

$$e[k] = x\left[k - \frac{1}{2}\right] \cdot \Big( x[k] - x[k - 1] \Big)$$

Why This Formula is Mathematical Genius

Let's trace what the algebra does across all possible symbol sequences:

Scenario Sample Values Gardner Error Output ($e[k]$) Loop Reaction
No Transition ($+1 \to +1$) $x[k] = +1$, $x[k-1] = +1$ $(+1 - 1) = 0 \implies \mathbf{e[k] = 0}$ No clock information. Do nothing.
Transition ($-1 \to +1$), Perfect Timing $x[k] = +1$, $x[k-1] = -1$, mid-point crosses zero: $x[k - 1/2] = 0$ $0 \cdot (+1 - (-1)) = \mathbf{e[k] = 0}$ Perfect lock. Clock is on target.
Transition ($-1 \to +1$), Sampled Late The mid-point was sampled after the zero crossing: $x[k - 1/2] > 0$ $(>0) \cdot (+2) \implies \mathbf{e[k] > 0}$ Positive error: tells loop to slow down.
Transition ($-1 \to +1$), Sampled Early The mid-point was sampled before the zero crossing: $x[k - 1/2] < 0$ $(<0) \cdot (+2) \implies \mathbf{e[k] < 0}$ Negative error: tells loop to speed up.
Non-Data-Aided Simplicity

Notice that the Gardner detector requires zero training preambles and zero knowledge of the transmitted bits! A single multiplication and subtraction computes the exact magnitude and sign of your clock drift on every bit transition.

4. The Software Clock Tracking Loop

In a complete receiver (such as GNU Radio or an optimized Zig kernel), the Gardner error drives a standard Phase-Locked Loop (PLL): 

[Raw Samples] ──► [Fractional Interpolator] ──► [On-time Sample x[k]]
                           ▲                               │
                           │                               ▼
                    [NCO Integrator] ◄── [PI Loop Filter] ◄── [Gardner Detector e[k]]

When the Gardner detector outputs error $e[k]$, a Proportional-Integral (PI) filter adjusts a Numerically Controlled Oscillator (NCO). Because the required sampling instant falls between discrete ADC samples, a polynomial interpolator (such as a 4-tap cubic Hermite or Farrow filter) reconstructs the exact value of the continuous wave at the optimal eye-opening instant!

Checking Your Mastery

1. What happens to the Gardner error metric e[k] when consecutive transmitted symbols do not change state (e.g. +1 followed by +1)?
  • The error metric saturates to the maximum positive floating point integer value.
  • The difference term (x[k] - x[k-1]) evaluates to zero, yielding zero timing error.
  • The fractional interpolator resets its internal tap weights to the initial state.
  • The loop filter forces the receiver to double its incoming ADC sampling frequency.
Correct! When there is no transition between consecutive symbols, $x[k] - x[k-1] \approx 0$, so $e[k] = 0$. Timing information can only be extracted from signal transitions across the zero crossing.
2. Why does a software SDR require a polynomial interpolator (such as Farrow) in its timing recovery loop?
  • Physical antennas cannot receive radio signals unless voltages are smoothed by cubic polynomials.
  • BEAM garbage collectors require streaming audio buffers to be aligned to power-of-two boundaries.
  • The optimal eye-opening sampling instant typically falls between discrete integer ADC sample points.
  • The Gardner detector produces non-linear harmonic distortion that must be filtered before bit slicing.
Correct! Because transmitter and receiver oscillators are asynchronous, the optimal peak of the eye diagram rarely lands exactly on an integer sample index ($n \in \mathbb{Z}$). The interpolator reconstructs the continuous signal at fractional sample offsets ($n + \mu$, where $0 \le \mu < 1$).
