Lesson 0011 Distributed SDR & Geolocation Reading time: 10 minutes

Clustering SDRs: Multilateration (TDOA) and Distributed BEAM Nodes

What happens when you deploy two Portenta X8 boards, each equipped with an RTL-SDR dongle and separated by a baseline distance (e.g., 500 meters or across town)?

You transform individual radio listeners into a distributed sensor aperture capable of:

  • Spatial Diversity: Overcoming multipath signal fades (if Node A sits in an RF null, Node B captures the packet).
  • Passive Emitter Geolocation (TDOA): Pinpointing the location of unknown or rogue radio transmitters without their cooperation.

1. The Physics of Time Difference of Arrival (TDOA)

Radio waves propagate through the atmosphere at the speed of light:

$$c \approx 3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s} = \mathbf{300\text{ meters per microsecond}} \quad (0.3\text{ meters/ns})$$

Suppose an unknown radio transmitter (e.g., an airplane, drone, or pirate ham station) transmits a signal at an unknown instant $t_0$. The wavefront reaches our two receivers at times $t_A$ and $t_B$:

$$t_A = t_0 + \frac{d_A}{c}, \qquad t_B = t_0 + \frac{d_B}{c}$$

Subtracting the two arrival times eliminates the unknown transmission instant $t_0$:

$$\Delta t = t_A - t_B = \frac{d_A - d_B}{c} \implies d_A - d_B = c \cdot \Delta t$$
The Hyperbolic Geolocation Line

In geometry, the locus of all 2D points where the difference in distances to two focal points ($|d_A - d_B|$) is constant forms a hyperbola.

With just two receivers, measuring $\Delta t$ proves that the transmitter sits somewhere along that specific hyperbolic curve on the map!

With three or four clustered receivers, multiple hyperbolas intersect, pinpointing the exact coordinates of the transmitter in 3D space—even if the transmitter never broadcasts its GPS location!

2. Measuring $\Delta t$: The Cross-Correlation Theorem

How do two Portenta nodes calculate the arrival time difference $\Delta t$ from raw I/Q baseband samples?

We compute the Cross-Correlation $R_{xy}(\tau)$ between the signal $x(t)$ captured by Node A and $y(t)$ captured by Node B:

$$R_{xy}(\tau) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) y^*(t - \tau) dt$$

By the cross-correlation theorem, this is computed at wire speed in the frequency domain:

$$R_{xy}(\tau) = \mathcal{F}^{-1} \Big\{ X(f) \cdot Y^*(f) \Big\}$$
  1. Node A computes the FFT of its I/Q burst: $X(f) = \text{FFT}(x)$.
  2. Node B computes the FFT of its I/Q burst: $Y(f) = \text{FFT}(y)$.
  3. Multiply $X(f)$ by the complex conjugate $Y^*(f)$ and take the Inverse FFT.
  4. The index of the maximum peak in the resulting array gives the exact sub-microsecond time delay $\Delta t$!

3. The Architecture: Distributed BEAM Cluster Across Portentas

In C, Python, or Go, clustering two embedded Linux boards requires setting up ZeroMQ, gRPC, Redis, or Kafka.

In Elixir / BEAM running on the Portenta X8's Cortex-A53 Linux cores, clustering is a native language primitive: 

# On Portenta Node A (192.168.1.10):
PORTENTA=1 iex --name node_a@192.168.1.10 --cookie sdr_secret -S mix

# On Portenta Node B (192.168.1.11):
PORTENTA=2 iex --name node_b@192.168.1.11 --cookie sdr_secret -S mix

Inside Elixir, connecting the nodes is a single function call: 

Node.connect(:"node_b@192.168.1.11")

# Both nodes join the distributed process group:
:pg.join(:sdr_cluster, self())

When Node B detects an RF burst or decodes a frame, it broadcasts an Erlang message across the cluster. The BEAM transparently serializes and transfers the binary over an encrypted TCP link with zero external broker infrastructure.

4. The Engineering Trap: The Nanosecond Synchronization Barrier

Here is the catch that trips up every junior SDR engineer:

The Cruel Clock Math

Because light travels 300 meters per microsecond:

  • A timing error of 1 microsecond ($\mu\text{s}$) creates a position error of 300 meters.
  • A standard Network Time Protocol (NTP) sync over Wi-Fi has 1 to 10 milliseconds of jitter $\implies \mathbf{300 \text{ to } 3,000 \text{ kilometers}}$ of error! NTP is completely useless for TDOA.

How to Synchronize Two Portenta X8s in Reality

Fortunately, the Portenta X8 provides two elegant hardware solutions:

Synchronization Method Implementation Mechanism Achieved Accuracy
GPS PPS Interrupt via STM32H7 Connect a $15 GPS module (u-blox) to a GPIO pin on each board's STM32H7. The GPS Pulse-Per-Second (PPS) pin fires an interrupt accurate to $\pm 20\text{ ns}$. The M7 timestamps the sample stream on every PPS edge. ± 20 nanoseconds (± 6 meters error)
Opportunistic Calibration (Reference Tower) Both SDRs tune to a known stationary broadcast tower (e.g. a local FM station or fixed airport beacon). Because the tower's distance is fixed and known, cross-correlating its signal continuously measures and subtracts the internal clock offset $\Delta t_{\text{clock}}$. ± 50 nanoseconds (± 15 meters error, zero extra hardware!)
Hardware PTP (IEEE 1588) Connect the two Portentas via Ethernet switch. The Portenta’s Ethernet PHY hardware stamps packets at the MAC layer. ± 100 nanoseconds (± 30 meters error)

Checking Your Mastery

1. If two clustered SDRs measure a signal arrival time difference of $\Delta t = 2.0\ \mu\text{s}$, what is the physical distance difference $(d_A - d_B)$ to the transmitter?
  • The distance difference equals precisely one hundred and fifty meters along the line.
  • The distance difference equals precisely six hundred meters along the hyperbolic locus.
  • The distance difference equals precisely three kilometers across the baseline antenna array.
  • The distance difference equals zero meters because radio waves travel at infinite velocity.
Correct! Using $d_A - d_B = c \cdot \Delta t$: $(300\text{ meters}/\mu\text{s}) \times 2.0\ \mu\text{s} = \mathbf{600\text{ meters}}$.
2. Why does computing cross-correlation in the frequency domain ($R_{xy} = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{X(f) \cdot Y^*(f)\}$) scale better than direct time-domain convolution?
  • The frequency domain eliminates the speed of light delay between the two receiving antennas.
  • Erlang process groups can only transmit complex numbers when formatted in polar coordinates.
  • FFT-based correlation reduces computational complexity from $O(N^2)$ to $O(N \log N)$ operations.
  • Complex conjugation physically forces the two asynchronous local oscillators into phase lock.
Correct! For a burst of $N$ samples (e.g. $N = 32,768$), direct time-domain cross-correlation requires $O(N^2) \approx 10^9$ operations. Using the FFT cross-correlation theorem takes $O(N \log N) \approx 5 \times 10^5$ operations—over **2,000 times faster**!
