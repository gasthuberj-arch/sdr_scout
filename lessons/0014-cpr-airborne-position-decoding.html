Compact Position Reporting (CPR): Non-Linear Spherical Coordinates & Disambiguation

When transmitting aircraft coordinates over a 1090 MHz Mode-S radio link, RF spectrum constraints force brutal trade-offs. An ADS-B Extended Squitter packet allocates exactly 34 bits for geographical coordinates: 17 bits for latitude and 17 bits for longitude.

Yet commercial aviation demands sub-5-meter positional resolution for precision runway approach, ADS-B Out surveillance, and TCAS collision avoidance. How does aviation achieve meter-scale accuracy across a global sphere inside a 34-bit budget without sending 64-bit IEEE-754 floating-point coordinates?

The answer is Compact Position Reporting (CPR) (ICAO Annex 10, RTCA DO-260B). CPR is an elegant fusion of spherical geometry, non-linear meridian compression, and the Vernier principle (discrete modulo disambiguation). In this lesson, we break down the spherical mathematics of CPR and analyze its concrete implementation in lib/sdr_scout/mode_s/cpr.ex.

1. The 34-Bit RF Budget: Why Floats and Linear Fixed-Point Fail

In an ADS-B DF17 frame, the 56-bit Message Extended (ME) field contains type-specific data. For Airborne Position messages (Type Codes 9 through 18), the bit allocation is strictly partitioned:

Field Bit Width Description
TC (Type Code) 5 bits Airborne Position category and barometric/GNSS altitude type
SS (Surveillance Status) 2 bits No condition, permanent alert, temporary alert, SPI
NIC_sb / SAF 1 bit Single antenna flag / navigation integrity supplement
ALT (Altitude) 12 bits 25-foot or 100-foot resolution altitude encoding
T (Time) 1 bit UTC synchronization status flag (0 = unsynchronized)
F (CPR Format) 1 bit 0 = Even frame, 1 = Odd frame
LAT-CPR 17 bits Raw encoded latitude fractional coordinate
LON-CPR 17 bits Raw encoded longitude fractional coordinate

Notice the constraint: exactly 17 bits each for latitude and longitude ($2^{17} = 131,072$ discrete states).

The Failure of Linear Fixed-Point Quantization

Suppose an engineer attempted a naive linear fixed-point mapping across the globe:

  • Latitude: Spanning $[-90^\circ, +90^\circ]$ ($180^\circ$ total). $$\Delta\text{Lat}_{\text{linear}} = \frac{180^\circ}{131,072} \approx 0.001373^\circ \approx 152.7\text{ meters } (501\text{ ft})$$
  • Longitude: Spanning $[-180^\circ, +180^\circ]$ ($360^\circ$ total). $$\Delta\text{Lon}_{\text{linear}} = \frac{360^\circ}{131,072} \approx 0.002747^\circ \approx 305.4\text{ meters at equator } (1,002\text{ ft})$$

A positional quantization error of $\pm 150$ to $300$ meters is catastrophic for terminal airspace separation. Two aircraft on parallel runways 750 feet apart would be indistinguishable!

The CPR Insight: Separate Zone from Fraction

Instead of encoding global absolute position in 17 bits, CPR divides the globe into discrete geographic zones. The 17-bit integer does not encode an absolute coordinate; it encodes the high-resolution fractional position inside a zone.

Because a latitude zone is only $6^\circ$ wide, the resolution is: $$\text{Resolution}_{\text{CPR}} = \frac{6^\circ}{131,072} \approx 0.00004578^\circ \approx 5.09\text{ meters } (16.7\text{ ft})$$

This delivers a 30× to 60× resolution improvement using the exact same 17-bit register. The challenge then becomes: How does the receiver know which zone the aircraft is in?

2. Spherical Geometry & The Vernier Latitude Grids

To resolve zone ambiguity without increasing packet size, CPR uses two alternating, coprime spatial grids, designated Even ($F=0$) and Odd ($F=1$). The aircraft transponder alternates between transmitting an Even frame and an Odd frame (typically every 0.5 seconds).

Latitude is partitioned into equal-width bands between the poles. The system defines 15 primary quadrants per hemisphere, producing:

  • Even Grid ($i = 0$): $N_B = 60$ latitude zones ($4 \times 15$). Zone width: $$\Delta\text{Lat}_0 = \frac{360^\circ}{60} = 6.0^\circ$$
  • Odd Grid ($i = 1$): $N_B - 1 = 59$ latitude zones ($4 \times 15 - 1$). Zone width: $$\Delta\text{Lat}_1 = \frac{360^\circ}{59} \approx 6.1016949^\circ$$

Notice that $\gcd(60, 59) = 1$. The number of zones is coprime!

The Mechanical Analogy: The Vernier Caliper

In a physical Vernier caliper, a fixed main scale with $1.0\text{ mm}$ graduations is paired with a sliding scale with $0.9\text{ mm}$ graduations. The difference ($0.1\text{ mm}$) creates a phase shift that uniquely identifies sub-millimeter displacement across the entire measurement range.

CPR applies this identical principle to the Earth's sphere. Over a full $360^\circ$ great circle, the Even grid contains exactly 60 zones, while the Odd grid contains exactly 59 zones:

$$60 \cdot \Delta\text{Lat}_0 = 59 \cdot \Delta\text{Lat}_1 = 360^\circ$$

Because the grids slip out of phase by exactly $\Delta\text{Lat}_1 - \Delta\text{Lat}_0$ per zone, every latitude on Earth has a unique phase difference between its Even and Odd fractional coordinates.

Global Latitude Disambiguation Formula

When an SDR receiver captures both an Even frame and an Odd frame from an aircraft, it extracts the 17-bit integers and normalizes them to the interval $[0, 1)$:

$$y_0 = \frac{\text{LAT\_CPR}_{\text{even}}}{131,072}, \qquad y_1 = \frac{\text{LAT\_CPR}_{\text{odd}}}{131,072}$$

The unique global latitude zone index $j$ is extracted analytically via integer rounding:

$$j = \left\lfloor 59 \cdot y_0 - 60 \cdot y_1 + \frac{1}{2} \right\rfloor$$

Once the integer zone index $j$ is recovered, the receiver computes the reconstructed latitude for both frames:

$$R_{\text{lat}, 0} = \Delta\text{Lat}_0 \cdot \left( (j \bmod 60) + y_0 \right)$$ $$R_{\text{lat}, 1} = \Delta\text{Lat}_1 \cdot \left( (j \bmod 59) + y_1 \right)$$

Because latitudes are conventionally referenced to the equator ($[-90^\circ, +90^\circ]$) rather than $[0^\circ, 360^\circ)$, values in the southern hemisphere wrap around $360^\circ$:

$$\text{Lat} = \begin{cases} R_{\text{lat}} - 360^\circ & \text{if } R_{\text{lat}} \ge 270^\circ \\ R_{\text{lat}} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

3. Meridian Convergence & The Non-Linear Longitude Function $NL(\text{lat})$

On a flat plane, dividing longitude into equal angular wedges would suffice. But Earth is a sphere. The physical distance $\Delta x$ along a parallel of latitude $\phi$ contracts with the cosine of latitude:

$$\Delta x = R_{\text{earth}} \cdot \cos(\phi) \cdot \Delta\lambda$$

At the equator ($\phi = 0^\circ$), $1^\circ$ of longitude is approximately $111.3\text{ km}$. At London or Frankfurt ($\phi \approx 51^\circ$), $1^\circ$ shrinks to $70\text{ km}$. At Svalbard ($\phi = 78^\circ$), it contracts to $23\text{ km}$. At the North Pole ($\phi = 90^\circ$), all meridians converge to a singular point of width zero.

If ADS-B used a fixed number of longitude zones worldwide, zone widths near the poles would shrink to centimeters, wasting bit capacity and causing mathematical instability during division.

Adaptive Longitudinal Mesh: $NL(\text{lat})$

CPR maintains a roughly constant physical zone width (~$360\text{ NM} \approx 666.7\text{ km}$) across all latitudes by dynamically reducing the number of longitude zones as latitude increases.

The number of longitude zones is given by the non-linear transition function $NL(\text{lat})$: $$NL(\text{lat}) = \left\lfloor \frac{2\pi}{\arccos\left( 1 - \frac{1 - \cos(\pi / (2 \cdot 15))}{\cos^2(\text{lat})} \right)} \right\rfloor$$

Evaluating $NL(\text{lat})$ yields a discrete step function:

Latitude Range $NL(\text{lat})$ (Even Zones) $NL(\text{lat}) - 1$ (Odd Zones) Angular Zone Width $\Delta\text{Lon}_0$
$0.0^\circ$ (Equator) 59 58 $360^\circ / 59 \approx 6.10^\circ$
$35.0^\circ$ (Mediterranean / Tokyo) 48 47 $360^\circ / 48 = 7.50^\circ$
$52.0^\circ$ (Berlin / London / Amsterdam) 36 35 $360^\circ / 36 = 10.00^\circ$
$70.0^\circ$ (Tromsø / Northern Alaska) 20 19 $360^\circ / 20 = 18.00^\circ$
$\ge 87.0^\circ$ (Polar Cap) 1 1 $360^\circ / 1 = 360.00^\circ$

Global Longitude Disambiguation

Once latitude has been reconstructed, the receiver computes $NL(\text{lat})$. For global decoding to be valid, both the Even and Odd frames must evaluate to the same $NL(\text{lat})$:

$$NL(R_{\text{lat}, 0}) \stackrel{?}{=} NL(R_{\text{lat}, 1})$$

If the two numbers differ, the aircraft crossed an $NL$ latitude boundary between the Even and Odd squitters. This condition is called a latitude zone straddle, and the receiver must discard the pair and wait for the next frame.

Assuming $NL$ is consistent:

  • For the Even frame, the number of longitude zones is $n_0 = \max(NL(\text{lat}), 1)$.
  • For the Odd frame, the number of longitude zones is $n_1 = \max(NL(\text{lat}) - 1, 1)$.

With normalized longitude fractions $x_0 = \frac{\text{LON\_CPR}_{\text{even}}}{131,072}$ and $x_1 = \frac{\text{LON\_CPR}_{\text{odd}}}{131,072}$, the global longitude zone index $m$ is:

$$m = \left\lfloor x_0 \cdot (NL - 1) - x_1 \cdot NL + \frac{1}{2} \right\rfloor$$

The reconstructed longitude corresponding to the newest received frame (e.g., if Even is newer) is:

$$\Delta\text{Lon} = \frac{360^\circ}{n_0}$$ $$\text{Lon} = \Delta\text{Lon} \cdot \left( (m \bmod n_0) + x_0 \right)$$

Finally, $\text{Lon}$ is normalized to $[-180^\circ, +180^\circ]$.

4. Global vs. Local Decoding: Bootstrapping vs. Tracking

An SDR receiver operates in two distinct CPR operational modes:

Attribute Global CPR Decoding Local CPR Decoding
Prerequisites Two alternating frames (1 Even + 1 Odd) Single frame (Even OR Odd) + known reference position
Time Window Strictly $\le 10$ seconds apart Continuous tracking (reference updated every few seconds)
Use Case Initial target acquisition / cold start High-rate position stream tracking (1–2 Hz)
Ambiguity Window Worldwide ($360^\circ \times 180^\circ$) Local radius within $\pm \frac{1}{2}\Delta\text{Lat} \approx 180\text{ NM}$
Failure Mode Packet drop delays fix; zone straddle rejection Zone slip if aircraft position jumps > 180 NM without fix

Why Does Global Decoding Require $\le 10$ Seconds?

An airliner at cruising speed travels at approximately 500 knots ($260\text{ m/s}$ or $\approx 0.93\text{ km/h}$). In 10 seconds, the aircraft travels at most $2.6\text{ km}$ ($1.4\text{ NM}$).

The mathematical tolerance of CPR allows for up to $\frac{1}{2} \Delta\text{Lat} \approx 3.0^\circ$ ($180\text{ NM}$) of displacement before the Vernier phase difference slips into an adjacent zone index. Enforcing a strict 10-second reception window guarantees that physical displacement during the transmission interval cannot induce an off-by-one zone error ($j \pm 1$).

How Local Decoding Works (Dead Reckoning)

Once an aircraft has been globally acquired, waiting for alternating Even/Odd pairs would cut the effective tracking update rate in half and make tracking vulnerable to packet drops.

In Local Decoding, the receiver uses its own known ground station coordinates (or the aircraft's previously decoded position) as a reference $(\text{Lat}_{\text{ref}}, \text{Lon}_{\text{ref}})$. Because the receiver already knows which coarse $6^\circ$ zone the aircraft resides in, a single 17-bit report (either Even or Odd) provides the exact 5-meter sub-zone offset:

$$j = \left\lfloor \frac{\text{Lat}_{\text{ref}}}{\Delta\text{Lat}} \right\rfloor + \left\lfloor \frac{\text{Lat}_{\text{ref}} \bmod \Delta\text{Lat}}{\Delta\text{Lat}} - y + \frac{1}{2} \right\rfloor$$ $$\text{Lat} = \Delta\text{Lat} \cdot (j + y)$$

This allows an SDR pipeline to emit immediate position updates for every single valid airborne position packet received.

5. Codebase Deep Dive: SdrScout.ModeS.CPR

Now let's inspect the actual implementation in lib/sdr_scout/mode_s/cpr.ex. 

defmodule SdrScout.ModeS.CPR do
  @d_lat_even 360.0 / 60.0  # 6.0 degrees
  @d_lat_odd 360.0 / 59.0   # 6.1016949... degrees

  def decode_global(even, odd) do
    # 17-bit CPR coordinates normalized to [0, 1)
    yz_0 = even.lat_cpr / 131_072.0
    yz_1 = odd.lat_cpr / 131_072.0
    xz_0 = even.lon_cpr / 131_072.0
    xz_1 = odd.lon_cpr / 131_072.0

    # 1. Compute latitude index j
    j = floor(59.0 * yz_0 - 60.0 * yz_1 + 0.5)

    r_lat_even = @d_lat_even * (rem_floor(j, 60) + yz_0)
    r_lat_odd = @d_lat_odd * (rem_floor(j, 59) + yz_1)

    r_lat_even = if r_lat_even >= 270.0, do: r_lat_even - 360.0, else: r_lat_even
    r_lat_odd = if r_lat_odd >= 270.0, do: r_lat_odd - 360.0, else: r_lat_odd

    # Check which message is newer
    {lat, nl, is_even_newer} =
      if even.time >= odd.time do
        {r_lat_even, nl(r_lat_even), true}
      else
        {r_lat_odd, nl(r_lat_odd), false}
      end

    if nl(r_lat_even) != nl(r_lat_odd) do
      {:error, :cpr_transition_straddle}
    else
      # 2. Compute longitude
      lon =
        if is_even_newer do
          ni = max(nl, 1)
          d_lon = 360.0 / ni
          m = floor(xz_0 * (nl - 1) - xz_1 * nl + 0.5)
          normalize_lon(d_lon * (rem_floor(m, ni) + xz_0))
        else
          ni = max(nl - 1, 1)
          d_lon = 360.0 / ni
          m = floor(xz_0 * (nl - 1) - xz_1 * nl + 0.5)
          normalize_lon(d_lon * (rem_floor(m, ni) + xz_1))
        end

      {:ok, {Float.round(lat, 5), Float.round(lon, 5)}}
    end
  end

The Systems Trap: Truncated Remainder vs. Floored Modulo

Look closely at line 115 in cpr.ex: 

defp rem_floor(a, b) do
  a - b * floor(a / b)
end
The C / Rust / Elixir Modulo Pitfall

In C (%), Rust (%), and Elixir (rem/2), the remainder operator is truncated towards zero, not floored towards negative infinity!

If $a = -1$ and $b = 60$:

  • Truncated remainder (C/Rust -1 % 60): evaluates to -1.
  • Floored modulo (CPR requirement $\text{mod}(a, b)$): evaluates to +59.

When an aircraft flies near the equator or south of $0^\circ$ latitude, $j$ can be negative. Using standard rem(j, 60) causes the reconstructed latitude to leap by hundreds of miles! In C or Rust, you must write ((a % b) + b) % b or a.rem_euclid(b). In Elixir, cpr.ex implements true floored modulo via rem_floor/2.

Summary & Engineering Takeaways

  • Bit Budgeting: 34 bits is insufficient for linear global coordinates at navigation precision ($< 10\text{ m}$), but ample when encoding fractional offsets within localized modular zones.
  • Vernier Geometry: Coprime zone divisions ($60$ vs. $59$) provide global uniqueness without central coordinates.
  • Spherical Metric: Meridian convergence ($\cos\phi$) necessitates non-linear zone reduction ($NL(\text{lat})$) to prevent polar singularities.
  • Systems Modulo: Always verify truncated remainder vs. floored modulo semantics when porting mathematical algorithms to C, Rust, or BEAM bytecode.
