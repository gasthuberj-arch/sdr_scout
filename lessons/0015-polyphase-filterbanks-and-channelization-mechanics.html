Polyphase Filterbanks: Channelization Mechanics, Commutators, and Multirate DSP

In high-throughput Software-Defined Radio systems, one operational scenario appears constantly: an RF front-end captures a wide swath of spectrum containing dozens or hundreds of independent narrowband transmissions.

Consider monitoring amateur radio activity:

  • Ingesting a 10 MHz slice of the 10-meter band (28.0 to 29.7 MHz) or VHF band to demultiplex 100 independent 100 kHz channels simultaneously (FM repeaters, DMR, APRS, CW, and digital modes).
  • Surveillance and search-and-rescue scanners scanning entire mobile bands for emergency beacons.
  • Satellite ground stations receiving frequency-division multiplexed (FDM) telemetry carriers across a wide transponder passband.

How do we ingest a continuous complex baseband stream at $f_s = 10\text{ MSPS}$ and split it into 100 isolated baseband streams at $f_{\text{out}} = 100\text{ kSPS}$ without saturating CPU memory buses or stalling real-time threads?

This lesson dissects the mathematical mechanics, multirate theorems, and systems architecture of the Polyphase Filterbank (PFB) Channelizer.

1. The Naive DDC Bank: The Arithmetic Wall

The classic textbook solution deploys a bank of $M$ independent Digital Down Converters (DDCs), one for each channel $k \in \{0, 1, \dots, M-1\}$:

  1. Mixer (Frequency Shift): Multiply incoming samples $x[n]$ by a complex carrier $e^{-j 2\pi f_k n / f_s}$ to translate channel $k$ to baseband ($0\text{ Hz}$).
  2. Channel Filter: Apply a sharp low-pass FIR filter $h[n]$ with $L$ taps to reject adjacent channel energy.
  3. Decimator: Downsample by factor $M$ ($[\downarrow M]$) to match the channel's Nyquist bandwidth ($f_{\text{out}} = f_s / M$).
The Absurd Arithmetic Cost

To achieve adjacent-channel rejection $>80\text{ dB}$ with a sharp transition band (e.g. $10\text{ kHz}$ transition width at $f_s = 10\text{ MHz}$), the prototype FIR filter requires at least $L = 800$ taps (filter depth $K = L/M = 8$).

Evaluating $M = 100$ parallel FIR filters at the full input rate $f_s = 10\text{ MSPS}$:

$$\text{Workload} = M \times L \times f_s = 100 \times 800 \times 10,000,000 = \mathbf{800 \times 10^9 \text{ MACs/sec}}$$

800 Giga-MACs per second! Crucially, after computing 800 billion multiplications, the downsampler immediately throws away 99 out of every 100 computed samples. Over 99% of CPU execution cycles and SIMD vector instructions are instantly discarded.

2. Why Naive Windowed FFTs Fail: The Spectral Leakage Trap

A common engineering instinct is: "Why run filters at all? Why not just collect $M = 100$ samples and take an $M$-point Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) repeatedly?"

An $M$-point Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) computed on non-overlapping blocks of $M$ samples:

$$X_k[m] = \sum_{n=0}^{M-1} x[m M + n] e^{-j \frac{2\pi}{M} k n}$$

Mathematically, this is identical to passing the signal through a bank of $M$ bandpass filters whose impulse response is a rectangular window of length $M$.

The discrete-time frequency response of a length-$M$ rectangular filter is the Dirichlet sinc kernel:

$$H_{\text{rect}}(\omega) = e^{-j \omega \frac{M-1}{2}} \frac{\sin(\omega M / 2)}{\sin(\omega / 2)}$$

This reveals two catastrophic flaws for channelization:

  • Disastrous Sidelobes (-13 dB): The first sidelobe of a rectangular window is only $-13.3\text{ dB}$ below the passband peak, decaying at a sluggish $-6\text{ dB/octave}$ ($1/f$). In an amateur radio band, an S9+40 dB (+10 dBm) FM repeater will blast through the $-13\text{ dB}$ sidelobes into neighboring channels, completely obliterating weak $-115\text{ dBm}$ digital signals (e.g. FT8, APRS, or DMR).
  • The Windowing Dilemma: Multiplying each $M$-sample block by a Hann or Blackman window reduces sidelobes (Hann gives $-31.5\text{ dB}$ peak sidelobes, Blackman gives $-58\text{ dB}$), but windowing dramatically widens the mainlobe. A Hann window doubles the mainlobe width to $4\pi / M$; a Blackman window triples it to $6\pi / M$. This causes severe passband droop and massive inter-channel crosstalk across adjacent bins.
The Fundamental Degree-of-Freedom Limit

An $M$-point FFT channelizer is fundamentally constrained to filter length $L = M$. You cannot independently control passband flatness, transition steepness, and stopband attenuation with only $M$ taps.

To achieve a brickwall response with $>80\text{ dB}$ attenuation and steep roll-off, a prototype filter must span $L = K \cdot M$ taps, where $K \ge 8$ to $16$ is the polyphase filter depth.

3. The Noble Identities of Multirate DSP

To eliminate the 99% wasted computation of filtering before downsampling, multirate signal processing relies on the Noble Identities.

The First Noble Identity (for decimation) states that filtering by an upsampled impulse response $G(z^M)$ followed by downsampling by $M$ is mathematically identical to downsampling by $M$ first, followed by filtering with $G(z)$ at the decimated sample rate:

$$[\text{Filter } G(z^M)] \longrightarrow [\downarrow M] \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad [\downarrow M] \longrightarrow [\text{Filter } G(z)]$$ 
(a) High-Rate Evaluation:
x[n] ──► [ G(z^M) ] ──► [ Downsample by M ] ──► y[m]   (Runs at f_s)

(b) Equivalent Low-Rate Evaluation (Noble Identity):
x[n] ──► [ Downsample by M ] ──► [ G(z) ] ──► y[m]     (Runs at f_s / M)

To apply this identity to our arbitrary prototype low-pass FIR filter $H(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{L-1} h[n] z^{-n}$ of length $L = K \cdot M$, we partition its impulse response into $M$ interleaved subsets:

The Polyphase Decomposition

We group the tap indices $n \in \{0, \dots, L-1\}$ modulo $M$: let $n = r M + k$, where $k \in \{0, 1, \dots, M-1\}$ represents the branch index and $r \in \{0, 1, \dots, K-1\}$ represents the tap index within that branch.

The transfer function $H(z)$ decomposes into $M$ polyphase components:

$$H(z) = \sum_{k=0}^{M-1} z^{-k} E_k(z^M)$$

where each polyphase sub-filter $E_k(z)$ has only $K = L/M$ taps:

$$E_k(z) = \sum_{r=0}^{K-1} h[r M + k] z^{-r}$$

By applying the Noble Identity to each branch, the delay chain $z^{-k}$ combined with the downsampler $[\downarrow M]$ transforms into an input commutator, and each sub-filter $E_k(z)$ operates strictly at the decimated output rate $f_s / M$!

4. The Complete PFB Architecture

The Polyphase Filterbank Channelizer integrates three stages into a single pipeline:

  1. The Commutator (Spinning Input Distributor): Distributes blocks of $M$ incoming high-rate samples into $M$ branch delay lines.
  2. Sub-Filter Convolutions: $M$ parallel FIR sub-filters, each of length $K = L/M$, executing at the low rate $f_s / M$.
  3. The $M$-point IFFT Synthesis Block: Simultaneously mixes all $M$ channel outputs to baseband ($0\text{ Hz}$).

Why the IFFT Acts as a Universal Parallel Mixer

Let $v_k[m]$ be the scalar output produced by branch sub-filter $E_k(z)$ at decimated time step $m$. To translate each channel $p \in \{0, \dots, M-1\}$ from its center frequency to $0\text{ Hz}$, the multirate derivation requires applying a discrete phase rotation $e^{j 2\pi p k / M}$.

Computing channel $p$'s output $Y_p[m]$:

$$Y_p[m] = \sum_{k=0}^{M-1} v_k[m] \, e^{j \frac{2\pi}{M} p k}$$

This equation is the definition of the $M$-point Inverse Discrete Fourier Transform (IDFT) of the sub-filter output vector $\mathbf{v}[m]$.

Instead of calculating $M$ independent complex mixers and filters, a single $M$-point Inverse Fast Fourier Transform synthesizes all $M$ channelized baseband streams simultaneously!

5. Computational Complexity Proof: $O(M \log M)$ vs $O(M^2)$

Let us evaluate the computational complexity per output epoch (one output sample per channel, produced every $M$ incoming samples):

Naive DDC Complexity

  • Mixers: $M$ channels $\times M$ samples $= M^2$ complex multiplications.
  • FIR Filtering: If evaluated after downsampling (polyphase evaluation without FFT): $M$ channels $\times L$ taps $= M \times (K \cdot M) = K M^2$ real-complex MACs.
  • Total Naive Operations: $\mathcal{O}(K M^2)$ per output block.

PFB Channelizer Complexity

  • Commutator: Circular buffer pointer decrement $\implies \mathcal{O}(1)$ pointer operations per sample ($\mathcal{O}(M)$ per block).
  • Sub-Filters: $M$ sub-filters, each computing $K$ MACs $\implies M \cdot K$ MACs per block.
  • Synthesis IFFT: An $M$-point radix-2 Cooley-Tukey IFFT requires $\frac{M}{2} \log_2 M$ complex multiplications per block.
  • Total PFB Operations: $K M + \frac{M}{2} \log_2 M = M \left( K + \frac{1}{2} \log_2 M \right)$ operations per block.

Normalizing per channel per output sample:

$$\text{Cost}_{\text{naive}} = K \cdot M \quad \text{vs} \quad \text{Cost}_{\text{PFB}} = K + \frac{1}{2} \log_2 M$$

6. Systems Architecture: Memory Layout, Cache Locality, and SIMD

An algorithm's theoretical $\mathcal{O}(M \log M)$ complexity can be completely degraded by cache misses and pipeline stalls if the memory layout is poorly structured.

The Strided Memory Anti-Pattern

In a naive C/Rust implementation, prototype filter taps are stored in sequential memory: 

// NAIVE: Prototype filter taps stored in linear order h[0], h[1], ...
float prototype_taps[K * M];

// Accessing tap r of branch k requires stride M:
float tap = prototype_taps[r * M + k]; // STRIDED MEMORY ACCESS!

With $M = 1024$ channels (4096 bytes of float), a stride of $M$ causes each tap load to cross a 4 KB memory page boundary or multiple 64-byte L1 cache lines. The CPU cache prefetcher fails completely, stalling execution on memory latency.

Cache-Aligned Polyphase Matrix Layout

In high-performance SDR engines (and native Zig/Rust modules), polyphase sub-filter coefficients must be reorganized into a contiguous 2D matrix: 

// CACHE-OPTIMAL: Transposed polyphase tap matrix
// taps[r][k] stores tap index r across all M channels contiguously!
alignas(64) float pfb_taps[K][M];
alignas(64) float delay_line[K][M];

Why is the [K][M] layout optimal?

  • SIMD Fused Multiply-Add (FMA): For each filter tap $r \in \{0, \dots, K-1\}$, vector registers can load 8 (AVX2 ymm) or 16 (AVX-512 zmm) contiguous channel coefficients and data samples in a single unaligned/aligned instruction: 
    // AVX-512 inner branch loop (16 channels per instruction)
for (size_t k = 0; k < M; k += 16) {
    __m512 v_acc = _mm512_setzero_ps();
    for (size_t r = 0; r < K; ++r) {
        __m512 v_taps = _mm512_load_ps(&pfb_taps[r][k]);
        __m512 v_data = _mm512_load_ps(&delay_line[r][k]);
        v_acc = _mm512_fmadd_ps(v_taps, v_data, v_acc);
    }
    _mm512_store_ps(&ifft_input[k], v_acc);
}
  • L1 Cache Residency: For $M = 128$ channels and $K = 8$ taps, the entire polyphase tap table consumes only $8 \times 128 \times 4\text{ bytes} = \mathbf{4\text{ KB}}$, fitting entirely inside the CPU's 32 KB L1 data cache.
  • Zero-Copy Commutator via Virtual Memory Mirroring: By allocating circular buffers with mirrored virtual memory pages (using Linux mmap double-mapping, as in GNU Radio's vm_circbuf), the commutator writes incoming $M$-sample chunks contiguously without pointer wrapping branches or modulo operations.

Checking Your Mastery

1. Why is an M-point windowed FFT inadequate for channelizing wideband SDR spectrum compared to a Polyphase Filterbank?
  • The discrete Fourier transform cannot process complex baseband I/Q values without converting them to polar coordinates.
  • The windowed FFT introduces non-linear phase distortions that prevent digital carrier recovery in phase-shift keying.
  • The FFT filter length is constrained to M taps, causing high sidelobe leakage (-13 to -32 dB) or severe mainlobe broadening.
  • The fast Fourier transform algorithm requires radix-2 block sizes that cannot be mapped onto physical SDR antenna feeds.
Correct! An $M$-point FFT channelizer is fundamentally locked to a prototype filter of length $L = M$. Rectangular windows suffer from $-13.3\text{ dB}$ sidelobes, while tapering windows (Hann/Blackman) double or triple the mainlobe width, causing passband droop and adjacent channel crosstalk. A PFB decouples the channel count from filter length by choosing $L = K \cdot M$ ($K \ge 8$).
2. According to the First Noble Identity of multirate DSP, what transformation allows the PFB sub-filters to execute at the decimated rate?
  • Filtering by H(z) before downsampling can be replaced by taking the continuous Hilbert transform of the analog RF signal.
  • Filtering by G(z^M) followed by decimation by M is algebraically identical to downsampling by M before filtering by G(z).
  • Decimating by factor M preserves all high-frequency spectral components without requiring anti-aliasing low-pass filters.
  • Computing the complex autocorrelation of the input sequence commutes with time-domain linear convolution across all taps.
Correct! The First Noble Identity proves that an upsampled filter $G(z^M)$ followed by a factor-$M$ downsampler is mathematically identical to downsampling first by $M$ and then filtering with $G(z)$ at the reduced sample rate $f_s / M$, eliminating 100% of discarded computations.
3. In an optimized C or Zig PFB implementation, why is the transposed polyphase coefficient matrix [K][M] preferred over strided access [K*M]?
  • It reduces the total number of floating-point arithmetic additions required by the Cooley-Tukey butterfly stages.
  • It eliminates the need for 64-byte memory alignment when loading vectors into AVX-512 floating-point registers.
  • It guarantees that all channel output samples are written directly to disk without passing through the operating system.
  • It places coefficients for the same tap index across channels contiguously in memory, maximizing SIMD FMA throughput.
Correct! Storing polyphase taps in a transposed $[K][M]$ matrix ensures that tap index $r$ across channels $k, k+1, \dots, k+15$ is contiguous in memory. This enables vector load instructions and Fused Multiply-Add (FMA) execution across channels without strided cache misses.
