Pulse Shaping, Intersymbol Interference, and Root-Raised Cosine

In textbook digital logic, bits are crisp, instantaneous square transitions: a high voltage represents $1$, and a low voltage represents $0$. We imagine sending a sequence of rectangular pulses through the ether and sampling them cleanly at the receiver.

If you attempt this on the airwaves, two physical realities immediately destroy your link:

  1. Spectral Splatter: The sharp corners of rectangular pulses generate infinite high-frequency harmonics ($\text{sinc}$ sidelobes) that spill into neighboring frequencies, creating severe Adjacent-Channel Interference (ACI) and violating regulatory emission masks.
  2. Intersymbol Interference (ISI): When you pass those pulses through physical bandlimited filters or dispersive multipath channels, the pulses smear and stretch in time. The energy of previous and future symbols bleeds directly into the sampling instant of the current symbol.

This lesson explores the mathematics and systems engineering of Pulse Shaping: how Harry Nyquist's 1928 zero-ISI criterion and the Root-Raised Cosine (RRC) filter solve both problems simultaneously while maximizing receiver Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR).

1. The Physics of Rectangular Pulses: Spectral Catastrophe

Consider an unshaped baseband symbol stream transmitting symbols $a_k \in \{-1, +1\}$ at a symbol rate $R_s = 1/T_s$ baud. Each symbol is held for duration $T_s$, modeled by the continuous rectangular pulse:

$$p_{\text{rect}}(t) = \Pi\left(\frac{t}{T_s}\right) = \begin{cases} 1, & |t| \le \frac{T_s}{2} \\ 0, & |t| > \frac{T_s}{2} \end{cases}$$

Taking the continuous-time Fourier transform reveals the frequency-domain spectrum $P(f)$:

$$P(f) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \Pi\left(\frac{t}{T_s}\right) e^{-j 2\pi f t} dt = T_s \frac{\sin(\pi f T_s)}{\pi f T_s} = T_s \text{sinc}(f T_s)$$

The Power Spectral Density (PSD) is proportional to $|P(f)|^2 = T_s^2 \text{sinc}^2(f T_s)$. This mathematical function carries severe physical consequences:

The -20 dB/Decade Disaster & Key Clicks

While the main lobe nulls fall at integer multiples of the symbol rate $f = \pm 1/T_s$, the envelope of the sidelobes decays only as:

$$|P(f)| \propto \frac{1}{\pi |f| T_s} \implies |P(f)|^2 \propto \frac{1}{f^2} \quad (-20\text{ dB per decade})$$

In amateur radio, this is the classic phenomenon of key clicks in continuous wave (CW) Morse transmission. Hard-switching an RF carrier on and off generates broadband clicks hundreds of kilohertz away from your operating frequency.

Under FCC Part 97 (§97.307) and ITU-R spectral regulations, transmitters must constrain emissions within tight spectral masks. A raw rectangular pulse requires infinite RF bandwidth; transmitting it at legal power levels immediately pollutes adjacent channels.

2. The Bandlimiting Trap: Intersymbol Interference (ISI)

To satisfy spectral masks, an intuitive reaction is to insert a sharp lowpass filter (e.g., an 8th-order Butterworth or elliptic filter) at the transmitter output.

However, Fourier duality dictates that sharp truncation in frequency creates infinite ringing in time. The impulse response of a brick-wall lowpass filter of bandwidth $B$ is a continuous $\text{sinc}(2Bt)$ function whose ripples extend out to $t = \pm \infty$.

When a stream of symbols $a_k$ is transmitted through an arbitrary bandlimited channel $p(t)$, the continuous received baseband waveform before noise is:

$$r(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} a_k p(t - k T_s)$$

At the receiver, the demodulator samples $r(t)$ at periodic strobe instants $t = m T_s + t_0$. Assuming optimal clock alignment ($t_0 = 0$):

$$r(m T_s) = a_m p(0) + \sum_{\substack{k=-\infty \\ k \ne m}}^{\infty} a_k p\big((m - k) T_s\big)$$
Term Expression Physical Meaning
Desired Symbol $a_m p(0)$ The target bit/symbol at the current sampling instant $m$.
Intersymbol Interference (ISI) $\sum_{k \ne m} a_k p((m - k) T_s)$ Ringing tails of all preceding and succeeding symbols decaying into sample $m$.
Noise Sample $n(m T_s)$ Thermal noise and receiver frontend distortion.

If $p((m - k) T_s) \ne 0$ for $k \ne m$, the sum of interfering tails can easily exceed the magnitude of $a_m p(0)$. When $a_m = +1$, a sequence of neighboring $-1$ symbols will push the sampled voltage below the decision threshold, causing an unrecoverable bit error—even in a noiseless channel ($n(t) = 0$).

3. Nyquist's First Criterion for Zero ISI

In his landmark 1928 paper "Certain Topics in Telegraph Transmission Theory", Harry Nyquist proved that we do not need the pulse $p(t)$ to be zero outside the symbol interval $[0, T_s]$. We only require that the pulse passes through zero at every non-zero integer multiple of the symbol period:

$$\boxed{p(k T_s) = \begin{cases} 1, & k = 0 \\ 0, & k \ne 0 \quad (k \in \mathbb{Z}) \end{cases}}$$

What does this condition require in the frequency domain? Sampling $p(t)$ at uniform rate $1/T_s$ generates periodic spectral replicas spaced by the symbol rate $R_s = 1/T_s$. Nyquist proved that the time-domain zero-crossing condition is mathematically equivalent to:

$$\boxed{\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} P\left(f - \frac{k}{T_s}\right) = T_s \quad \text{for all } f}$$

The shifted spectral replicas of the pulse must sum to a flat constant across all frequencies.

The Minimum Bandwidth Solution: The Ideal Sinc Pulse

The absolute minimum continuous bandwidth that can satisfy Nyquist's criterion is a brick-wall lowpass filter extending from $-R_s/2$ to $+R_s/2$:

$$P_{\min}(f) = \begin{cases} T_s, & |f| \le \frac{1}{2 T_s} \\ 0, & |f| > \frac{1}{2 T_s} \end{cases}$$

This theoretical boundary is the Nyquist Bandwidth:

$$B_{\min} = \frac{R_s}{2} = \frac{1}{2 T_s}\text{ Hz}$$

Taking the inverse Fourier transform yields the ideal Nyquist pulse in the time domain:

$$p(t) = \text{sinc}\left(\frac{t}{T_s}\right) = \frac{\sin(\pi t / T_s)}{\pi t / T_s}$$
Why the Ideal Sinc Pulse is Unusable in Real Systems

Every digital communications textbook introduces the sinc pulse as the theoretical optimum. In practical SDR implementations, it is catastrophic:

  • Non-Causality & Truncation Ringing: A sinc pulse extends from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$. Truncating it to a practical FIR filter length introduces severe Gibbs ripple that destroys the exact zero-crossings.
  • Fragility to Timing Jitter ($1/t$ Decay): Sinc tails decay extremely slowly, proportional to $1/t$. If the receiver clock suffers from a minute timing error $\Delta t$, the sampled ISI evaluates to: $$\text{ISI} \approx \sum_{k \ne 0} a_k \frac{\sin(\pi (k + \Delta t / T_s))}{\pi (k + \Delta t / T_s)} \approx \frac{\sin(\pi \Delta t / T_s)}{\pi} \sum_{k \ne 0} \frac{(-1)^k a_k}{k}$$ Because the harmonic series $\sum 1/k$ diverges, an alternating sequence of symbols ($+1, -1, +1, -1\dots$) causes the accumulated ISI to diverge to infinity! A 1% clock phase error can close the receiver eye completely.

4. The Raised Cosine (RC) Filter & Excess Bandwidth

To eliminate the catastrophic $1/t$ decay while preserving Nyquist's zero-ISI property, we introduce excess bandwidth beyond the Nyquist minimum $B_0 = 1/(2T_s)$.

Instead of a sharp rectangular brick wall in frequency, we roll off the transition band smoothly using a raised cosine function that possesses odd symmetry around the Nyquist frequency $f_N = 1/(2T_s)$:

$$H_{RC}(f) = \begin{cases} T_s, & 0 \le |f| \le \frac{1-\alpha}{2T_s} \\ \frac{T_s}{2} \left[ 1 + \cos\left( \frac{\pi T_s}{\alpha} \left( |f| - \frac{1-\alpha}{2T_s} \right) \right) \right], & \frac{1-\alpha}{2T_s} < |f| \le \frac{1+\alpha}{2T_s} \\ 0, & |f| > \frac{1+\alpha}{2T_s} \end{cases}$$

The parameter $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ is the roll-off factor (also called the excess bandwidth parameter):

  • $\alpha = 0$: Reverts to the ideal brick-wall sinc filter with minimum bandwidth $B = R_s / 2$.
  • $\alpha = 1.0$: Full 100% excess bandwidth; the spectrum extends to $B = R_s$.
  • The total occupied single-sided RF baseband bandwidth is: $$\boxed{B_{RC} = \frac{R_s}{2}(1 + \alpha)}$$

Impulse Response and the Fast $1/t^3$ Decay

Computing the inverse Fourier transform of $H_{RC}(f)$ yields:

$$\boxed{h_{RC}(t) = \text{sinc}\left(\frac{t}{T_s}\right) \cdot \frac{\cos\left(\frac{\pi \alpha t}{T_s}\right)}{1 - \left(\frac{2\alpha t}{T_s}\right)^2}}$$

Notice the two factors in $h_{RC}(t)$:

  1. $\text{sinc}(t/T_s)$: Guarantees exact zero-crossings at every integer symbol instant $t = k T_s$ ($k \ne 0$), satisfying Nyquist's First Criterion.
  2. $\frac{\cos(\pi \alpha t / T_s)}{1 - (2\alpha t / T_s)^2}$: Introduces an asymptotic decay factor of $1/t^2$. Combined with the $1/t$ decay of the sinc function, the overall impulse response decays as: $$|h_{RC}(t)| \propto \frac{1}{t^3} \quad \text{for } |t| \gg T_s$$
Why $1/t^3$ Decay Saves Practical SDRs

Because $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k^3} = \zeta(3) \approx 1.202$ converges rapidly, any residual timing offset $\Delta t$ produces strictly bounded, negligible ISI. Truncating the FIR filter to just 6 or 8 symbol periods captures over 99.99% of the total pulse energy without generating observable spectral splatter.

5. The Root-Raised Cosine (RRC) Split

If the Raised Cosine filter provides zero ISI, why don't we simply apply $H_{RC}(f)$ as an FIR pulse-shaping filter at the transmitter and use a wideband lowpass filter at the receiver?

The answer lies in the fundamental theorem of digital communications: the Matched Filter Theorem (established in Lesson 4).

The Matched Filter Dilemma

In an Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN) channel, the receiver filter $H_{RX}(f)$ that maximizes the output Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) at the sampling instant must be the complex conjugate of the received pulse shape:

$$H_{RX}(f) = H_{TX}^*(f)$$

Now observe the contradiction:

  • If we place the full Raised Cosine filter at the transmitter ($H_{TX}(f) = H_{RC}(f)$), then a matched receiver must also apply $H_{RX}(f) = H_{RC}(f)$. The end-to-end composite channel response would be: $$H_{\text{total}}(f) = H_{TX}(f) \cdot H_{RX}(f) = H_{RC}^2(f) \ne H_{RC}(f)$$ $H_{RC}^2(f)$ does not satisfy the Nyquist zero-ISI criterion! The squared cosine roll-off destroys the odd symmetry around $f_N$, introducing severe self-ISI.
  • If instead the receiver uses a wideband flat filter to avoid altering $H_{RC}(f)$, it admits out-of-band noise, drastically reducing the receiver SNR and wasting link margin.

The Geometric Split: Root-Raised Cosine

To satisfy both the Matched Filter Theorem (optimal SNR) and Nyquist's First Criterion (zero ISI), we split the frequency response equally between transmitter and receiver:

$$\boxed{H_{TX}(f) = \sqrt{H_{RC}(f)} \equiv H_{RRC}(f)}$$ $$\boxed{H_{RX}(f) = \sqrt{H_{RC}(f)} \equiv H_{RRC}(f)}$$

Under this symmetric architecture:

$$H_{\text{total}}(f) = H_{TX}(f) \cdot H_{channel}(f) \cdot H_{RX}(f) = \sqrt{H_{RC}(f)} \cdot 1 \cdot \sqrt{H_{RC}(f)} = H_{RC}(f)$$ 
[Transmitter]                                      [Channel]                  [Receiver]
Symbols a[k] ──► [Upsampler ↑L] ──► [RRC Filter] ──────► [AWGN Channel] ──────► [RRC Matched Filter] ──► [Sampler t=kTs] ──► a^[k]
                                     H_TX(f)=√H_RC                              H_RX(f)=√H_RC
                                                                         Overall Cascade = H_RC(f) (Zero ISI!)
Vital Subtlety: RRC Alone Has Non-Zero ISI!

An isolated Root-Raised Cosine pulse $h_{RRC}(t)$ does not satisfy Nyquist's zero-ISI criterion. If you probe the output of the transmitter RRC filter directly on an oscilloscope, you will see significant intersymbol interference at integer multiples of $T_s$.

Zero ISI is achieved only after the received signal has passed through the receiver's matched RRC filter:

$$h_{\text{composite}}(t) = h_{RRC}(t) * h_{RRC}(t) = h_{RC}(t)$$

Continuous RRC Impulse Response Equation

The analytical impulse response $h_{RRC}(t)$ of a Root-Raised Cosine filter is given by:

$$\boxed{h_{RRC}(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{T_s}} \frac{\sin\left(\pi \frac{t}{T_s}(1-\alpha)\right) + 4\alpha \frac{t}{T_s}\cos\left(\pi \frac{t}{T_s}(1+\alpha)\right)}{\pi \frac{t}{T_s} \left(1 - \left(4\alpha \frac{t}{T_s}\right)^2\right)}}$$

When implementing this equation in C, Rust, or Zig, notice the two removable singularities:

  • At the pulse center $t = 0$: $$h_{RRC}(0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{T_s}} \left( 1 - \alpha + \frac{4\alpha}{\pi} \right)$$
  • At $t = \pm \frac{T_s}{4\alpha}$ (where the denominator evaluates to zero): applying L'Hôpital's rule yields: $$h_{RRC}\left(\pm \frac{T_s}{4\alpha}\right) = \frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{2 T_s}} \left[ \left(1 + \frac{2}{\pi}\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4\alpha}\right) + \left(1 - \frac{2}{\pi}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4\alpha}\right) \right]$$

Eye Parameter Physical Cause DSP / Receiver Impact
Eye Height (Vertical Aperture) Total signal amplitude minus accumulated ISI at optimal sampling instant $t = k T_s$. Direct measure of noise margin. A smaller eye height requires higher SNR to maintain a target Bit Error Rate (BER).

Visualizing the Trade-off: $\alpha = 0.2$ vs. $\alpha = 0.5$ vs. $\alpha = 1.0$

Choosing $\alpha$ is a fundamental engineering compromise between spectral density and implementation margin:

  • $\alpha = 0.2$ (Tight Spectrum): High spectral efficiency ($B = 1.2 \times R_s / 2$). However, the eye diagram has a narrower opening, substantial overshoot (ringing tails), and steep transition slopes. Requires high-precision timing recovery and linear PAs. Used in satellite communications (DVB-S2) and amateur QO-100 DATV.
  • $\alpha = 0.35 - 0.5$ (Balanced): Moderate excess bandwidth ($35\text{–}50\%$). Broad eye opening, low timing sensitivity, moderate overshoot. Standard in terrestrial digital radio, cellular (3G/LTE), and amateur digital voice (DMR $\alpha = 0.36$).
  • $\alpha = 1.0$ (Wide Eye): Consumes double the Nyquist bandwidth ($B = R_s$), but provides the maximum possible eye height and width. Zero-crossings are rock solid, making clock recovery trivial.

7. Systems Architecture: Implementing Discrete RRC Filtering

In an SDR baseband pipeline (such as GNU Radio, a C/Rust DSP engine, or an Elixir/Zigler NIF), pulse shaping is implemented using discrete multirate filtering.

To shape digital symbols into a continuous-like baseband waveform, the symbol stream must first be upsampled to an intermediate sample rate $f_s = L \cdot R_s$, where $L$ is the number of samples per symbol (typically $L = 4, 8$, or $16$):

  1. Zero-Stuffing (Upsampling by $L$): Insert $L - 1$ zeros between consecutive symbols $a[k]$: $$x[m] = \begin{cases} a[m / L], & m \bmod L = 0 \\ 0, & m \bmod L \ne 0 \end{cases}$$
  2. FIR Convolution: Convolve the zero-stuffed stream with a discrete truncated RRC filter $h[n]$: $$y[m] = \sum_{n=-N}^{N} x[m - n] \cdot h[n]$$

Filter Truncation & Filter Span

A discrete RRC filter is parameterized by its symbol span $N_{\text{span}}$ (the number of symbol periods spanned by the filter on each side of the center tap). The total number of FIR filter taps is:

$$N_{\text{taps}} = 2 \cdot N_{\text{span}} \cdot L + 1$$

For example, with $N_{\text{span}} = 6$ and $L = 4$ samples/symbol, $N_{\text{taps}} = 2 \times 6 \times 4 + 1 = 49$ taps. Choosing $N_{\text{span}} \ge 6$ ensures that the out-of-band spectral attenuation exceeds $60\text{ dB}$, satisfying emission masks.

The Polyphase Decomposition Breakthrough

Notice a massive computational inefficiency in direct convolution: because $x[m]$ was formed by zero-stuffing, $(L - 1)$ out of every $L$ samples in the buffer are zero! A naive FIR filter spends $75\%$ (for $L=4$) to $87.5\%$ (for $L=8$) of its CPU cycles multiplying filter coefficients by zero!

The Polyphase Interpolator Architecture

By decomposing the $N_{\text{taps}}$-length prototype filter $h[n]$ into $L$ smaller sub-filters (phases) $p_\ell[k] = h[k L + \ell]$ for $\ell \in \{0, 1, \dots, L - 1\}$, we commute decimation before multiplication: 

                 ┌──► [Sub-filter p_0[k]] ──► y[m*L + 0]
                 ├──► [Sub-filter p_1[k]] ──► y[m*L + 1]
Symbols a[k] ────┼──► [Sub-filter p_2[k]] ──► y[m*L + 2]
                 └──► [Sub-filter p_3[k]] ──► y[m*L + 3]
                 (Each sub-filter has only 2*N_span + 1 taps!)

Every input symbol $a[k]$ is passed into the sub-filters without inserting a single zero. For each input symbol, the polyphase engine outputs $L$ continuous baseband samples. This eliminates 100% of the useless zero-multiplications and maps directly to SIMD vector registers (AVX2/NEON FMA instructions).
