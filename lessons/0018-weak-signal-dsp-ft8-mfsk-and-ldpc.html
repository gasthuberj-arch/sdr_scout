Weak-Signal DSP: FT8, Costas Arrays, and Coherent Demodulation at -24 dB SNR

In high-speed communication systems like ADS-B, Wi-Fi, or cellular LTE, receivers operate at high Signal-to-Noise Ratios (often $+10\text{ dB}$ to $+30\text{ dB}$). Signal pulses tower over the thermal noise floor, and detection is largely an exercise in high-throughput thresholding and preamble correlation.

In weak-signal amateur radio (HAM), the physical reality is inverted. Protocols like FT8 (developed by Joe Taylor, K1JT, and Steve Franke, K9AN) operate routinely at $-20\text{ dB}$ to $-24\text{ dB}$ SNR. At these levels, the signal is invisible on a conventional analog S-meter and completely inaudible to human ears—buried deep inside the Gaussian thermal noise hiss.

Yet FT8 reliably achieves near-$100\%$ error-free packet reception across trans-continental ionospheric paths with milliwatts of transmit power. How is this mathematically and computationally possible?

In this lesson, we will deconstruct the weak-signal DSP pipeline:

  • The Information Theory Frontier: Shannon-Hartley capacity in the sub-zero SNR regime.
  • Physical Layer Framing: 8-MFSK continuous-phase modulation with $6.25\text{ Hz}$ tone spacing.
  • 2D Synchronization: Algebraic Costas arrays that eliminate time-frequency ambiguity sidelobes.
  • Discrete Spectral Demodulation: Matching observation duration to DFT bin resolution.
  • Error Control Coding: LDPC $(174, 91)$ belief propagation on bipartite Tanner graphs.

1. The Shannon-Hartley Theorem and the Negative-SNR Frontier

A pervasive misconception in radio engineering is that communication becomes impossible once signal power drops below noise power ($\text{SNR} < 0\text{ dB}$, or $S/N < 1$). Claude Shannon's landmark 1948 channel capacity theorem proves otherwise:

$$C = B \log_2\left(1 + \frac{S}{N}\right) = B \log_2(1 + \text{SNR}) \quad [\text{bits/second}]$$

Here, $B$ is channel bandwidth in Hertz, $S$ is average received signal power in Watts, and $N = N_0 B$ is the total noise power within bandwidth $B$, with $N_0$ representing single-sided noise spectral density ($\text{W/Hz}$).

The Asymptotic Wideband Limit

When $\text{SNR} \ll 1$ (deep sub-zero decibels), we apply the first-order Taylor expansion $\ln(1 + x) \approx x$ for $x \to 0$:

$$\log_2(1 + \text{SNR}) = \frac{\ln(1 + \text{SNR})}{\ln 2} \approx \frac{\text{SNR}}{\ln 2} \approx 1.4427 \cdot \text{SNR}$$

Substituting this into the capacity equation:

$$C \approx B \cdot \frac{S}{N_0 B \ln 2} = \frac{S}{N_0 \ln 2}$$

Notice the profound consequence: channel capacity does not collapse to zero when SNR becomes negative! Capacity becomes fundamentally power-limited rather than bandwidth-limited. You can communicate reliably over any channel with negative SNR, provided you trade time and bandwidth.

The Shannon Bound on Energy per Bit

If we transmit at rate $R = C$ bits per second, the energy per information bit is $E_b = S / C$. Substituting $C \approx \frac{S}{N_0 \ln 2}$ yields the famous absolute Shannon limit:

$$\frac{E_b}{N_0} \ge \ln 2 \approx 0.693 \implies 10 \log_{10}(\ln 2) = \mathbf{-1.59\text{ dB}}$$

No modulation or coding scheme can achieve error-free communication below $E_b / N_0 = -1.59\text{ dB}$. Modern weak-signal protocols like FT8 operate within $1.5\text{ dB}$ to $2.5\text{ dB}$ of this theoretical limit.

The Amateur Radio SNR Metric: $B_{\text{ref}} = 2500\text{ Hz}$

In amateur radio protocols (WSJT-X suite), SNR is by international convention referenced to a standard Single Sideband (SSB) voice bandwidth of $B_{\text{ref}} = 2500\text{ Hz}$:

$$\text{SNR}_{2500} = \frac{S}{N_{2500}} = \frac{S}{N_0 \cdot 2500}$$

When an FT8 receiver reports an SNR of $-24\text{ dB}$, the total signal power across the channel is:

$$\text{SNR}_{\text{linear}} = 10^{-24 / 10} = 10^{-2.4} \approx 0.00398$$

The signal is over 250 times weaker than the ambient thermal noise in a voice passband!

Processing Gain via Narrowband Bins

While the reference voice channel is $2500\text{ Hz}$ wide, an FT8 tone occupies a discrete bandwidth of only $\Delta f = 6.25\text{ Hz}$. When our DSP downsamples and runs a matched filter or Discrete Fourier Transform over the symbol period, we collect noise only within that $6.25\text{ Hz}$ sub-band.

The resulting processing gain (or bandwidth narrowing gain) is:

$$G_p = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{B_{\text{ref}}}{\Delta f}\right) = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{2500}{6.25}\right) = 10 \log_{10}(400) = \mathbf{+26.02\text{ dB}}$$

Now compute the effective SNR inside the detection bin:

$$\text{SNR}_{\text{bin}} = \text{SNR}_{2500} + G_p = -24.00\text{ dB} + 26.02\text{ dB} = \mathbf{+2.02\text{ dB}}$$

Inside the discrete $6.25\text{ Hz}$ tone bin, the signal is actually $2\text{ dB}$ above the noise floor! By stretching the transmission time to $12.64\text{ seconds}$ to convey 77 payload bits, FT8 converts an impossible wideband negative SNR into a solvable narrowband detection problem.

2. FT8 Physical Layer: Continuous-Phase 8-MFSK

FT8 uses 8-ary Multiple Frequency-Shift Keying (8-MFSK). Each transmitted symbol selects one of 8 discrete audio tones, conveying $\log_2(8) = 3$ bits per symbol.

Orthogonality in Non-Coherent Detection

Ionospheric propagation introduces unpredictable phase shifts, Doppler spread, and multipath fading. Maintaining phase coherence over 12.64 seconds across the HF spectrum is impractical. FT8 therefore relies on non-coherent detection.

Two complex tones $s_m(t) = e^{j 2\pi f_m t}$ and $s_n(t) = e^{j 2\pi f_n t}$ are mutually orthogonal over an observation window $T_s$ if their inner product vanishes for all $m \ne n$:

$$\langle s_m, s_n \rangle = \int_0^{T_s} e^{j 2\pi f_m t} e^{-j 2\pi f_n t} dt = \int_0^{T_s} e^{j 2\pi (f_m - f_n) t} dt$$

Evaluating this integral when $f_m - f_n = k \Delta f$:

$$\int_0^{T_s} e^{j 2\pi k \Delta f t} dt = T_s \left( \frac{e^{j 2\pi k \Delta f T_s} - 1}{j 2\pi k \Delta f T_s} \right) = T_s e^{j \pi k \Delta f T_s} \operatorname{sinc}(k \Delta f T_s)$$

The function $\operatorname{sinc}(x) = \frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x}$ has zeros at all non-zero integers $x \in \mathbb{Z} \setminus \{0\}$. For non-coherent orthogonality regardless of initial phase:

$$\Delta f \cdot T_s = 1 \implies \Delta f = \frac{1}{T_s} = \frac{1}{0.160\text{ s}} = \mathbf{6.25\text{ Hz}}$$

This is the minimum frequency separation that guarantees zero crosstalk between adjacent tones under non-coherent envelope detection.

3. Synchronization with Costas Arrays

Before an FT8 receiver can demodulate data symbols, it must resolve two unknowns in the presence of overwhelming noise:

  1. Time offset ($\tau$): When did the packet start? (Transmissions align to 15-second UTC boundaries, but clock jitter and propagation delay introduce up to $\pm 1-2\text{ s}$ uncertainty).
  2. Frequency offset ($f_0$): What is the exact audio carrier frequency? (Transceivers drift by tens of Hertz).

In high-SNR systems, periodic sync preambles or single-frequency pilot tones suffice. In weak-signal systems, a single tone or periodic chirp exhibits massive sidelobes in the 2D time-frequency plane. A time shift can be mistaken for a frequency shift, causing cross-correlation false alarms.

What is a Costas Array?

A Costas array (named after John P. Costas) is an $N \times N$ permutation matrix where all displacement vectors between pairs of points are strictly unique.

Mathematically, consider a sequence of $N$ frequency tones $C = [c_0, c_1, \dots, c_{N-1}]$ where each $c_k \in \{0, 1, \dots, N-1\}$ is a permutation of the integers $0$ to $N-1$. $C$ is a Costas array if and only if:

$$\forall \Delta t \in \{1, 2, \dots, N-1\}, \quad \text{the differences } \Delta f_k = c_{k + \Delta t} - c_k \text{ are all distinct.}$$

In FT8, a 7-symbol Costas sequence is used:

$$\mathbf{C} = [3, 1, 4, 0, 6, 5, 2]$$

The 2D Ambiguity Function

The aperiodic auto-ambiguity function between the transmitted Costas sequence and a replica shifted by time $\tau$ and frequency $\nu$ is:

$$\chi(\tau, \nu) = \sum_{k} \delta(C_{k+\tau}, C_k + \nu)$$

Because all difference vectors are distinct, for any non-zero shift $(\tau, \nu) \ne (0, 0)$, at most one single tone coincidence can ever occur:

$$\chi(\tau, \nu) \le 1 \quad \forall (\tau, \nu) \ne (0, 0), \qquad \chi(0, 0) = 7$$

The auto-correlation peak is $7$, while every cross-ambiguity sidelobe is bounded by $\le 1$. This yields a $7:1$ peak-to-sidelobe ratio ($8.45\text{ dB}$) in the 2D search grid from just 7 symbols!

Why Three Costas Arrays?

FT8 frames do not place the Costas array solely at the preamble. Instead, the 7-symbol sequence is inserted at three distinct positions: 

[Costas 7] --- [Data 29] --- [Costas 7] --- [Data 29] --- [Costas 7]
(Symbols 0..6)   (Symbols 7..35)  (Symbols 36..42)  (Symbols 43..71) (Symbols 72..78)

This tripartite distribution ($3 \times 7 = 21$ total sync symbols) serves three vital engineering goals:

  • Cumulative Detection Peak: Summing the three sync blocks yields an aggregate correlation peak of $21$, pushing the combined synchronization peak well above noise at $-24\text{ dB}$ SNR.
  • Linear Frequency Drift Tracking ($\dot{f} = df/dt$): Amateur HF signals frequently drift during the 12.64-second transmission due to crystal oscillator warm-up or Doppler shifts from moving ionospheric layers. Having sync points at $t = 0\text{ s}$, $t = 6.0\text{ s}$, and $t = 12.0\text{ s}$ allows the receiver to estimate and compensate for linear frequency drift.
  • Sample Clock Drift Tracking ($dt/dt$): Discrepancies between the transmitter's and receiver's audio ADC sample clocks cause time-scale dilation or compression. Comparing the timing of the first and last Costas bursts reveals the exact sample clock drift rate.

4. Demodulation via Discrete Fourier Transform

In typical SDR or sound-card processing pipelines, audio baseband is sampled at $f_s = 12000\text{ Hz}$.

To demodulate FT8, the sample stream is segmented into blocks corresponding to the symbol period $T_s = 160\text{ ms}$:

$$N_{\text{samp}} = f_s \cdot T_s = 12000\text{ samples/sec} \times 0.160\text{ sec} = \mathbf{1920\text{ samples}}$$

The frequency resolution of an $N$-point Discrete Fourier Transform across 1920 samples is:

$$\Delta f_{\text{bin}} = \frac{f_s}{N_{\text{samp}}} = \frac{12000}{1920} = \mathbf{6.25\text{ Hz}}$$

Notice the perfect mathematical match: one DFT bin corresponds exactly to one FT8 tone step ($\Delta f = 6.25\text{ Hz}$).

Non-Coherent Energy Extraction

For each candidate symbol interval $k \in \{0, \dots, 78\}$, the receiver computes the magnitude-squared energy across the 8 candidate tone bins $m \in \{0, \dots, 7\}$ centered at base carrier frequency $f_0$:

$$E(k, m) = \left| X_k(f_0 + m \Delta f) \right|^2 = \left| \sum_{n=0}^{N_{\text{samp}}-1} x[k N_{\text{samp}} + n] \, e^{-j \frac{2\pi (f_0 + m \Delta f) n}{f_s}} \right|^2$$

In high-performance C or Zig implementations, computing full 1920-point FFTs across hundreds of candidate frequencies is wasteful. Receivers often use sliding FFTs, batched SIMD Goertzel filters, or polyphase filter banks to evaluate only the active $50\text{ Hz}$ passband of interest.

Spectrogram Matrix Construction

Demodulating an FT8 frame produces a $79 \times 8$ real-valued energy matrix $\mathbf{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{79 \times 8}$. Rows $0..6$, $36..42$, and $72..78$ are matched against the Costas sequence to refine $\tau$ and $f_0$. The remaining $58$ rows represent candidate data symbols.

5. Forward Error Correction: LDPC (174, 91)

Raw tone detection at $-24\text{ dB}$ has a symbol error rate exceeding $40\%$. No communication can occur without powerful Forward Error Correction (FEC).

FT8 uses a systematic Low-Density Parity-Check (LDPC) code, designated as $\mathbf{\text{LDPC}(174, 91)}$:

  • Payload ($K$): $77$ bits of user payload (source callsign, destination callsign, grid locator or signal report) $+ 14$ bits of Cyclic Redundancy Check (CRC-14) $= \mathbf{91\text{ message bits}}$.
  • Codeword Length ($N$): $58\text{ data symbols} \times 3\text{ bits/symbol} = \mathbf{174\text{ coded bits}}$.
  • Parity Bits ($M = N - K$): $174 - 91 = \mathbf{83\text{ parity-check equations}}$.
  • Code Rate ($R$): $R = 91 / 174 \approx \mathbf{0.523}$ (roughly rate $1/2$).

The Parity-Check Matrix $H$ and Tanner Graph

The code is defined by a binary parity-check matrix $\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{F}_2^{83 \times 174}$ such that for any valid codeword $\mathbf{c} \in \{0, 1\}^{174}$:

$$\mathbf{H} \mathbf{c}^T = \mathbf{0} \pmod 2$$

The term Low-Density signifies that $\mathbf{H}$ is exceptionally sparse. Each row has an average of only $7$ ones (check degree $d_c \approx 7$), and each column has an average of $3$ ones (variable degree $d_v \approx 3$).

This defines a bipartite Tanner graph with two sets of vertices:

  • 174 Variable Nodes (VNs): Representing the transmitted code bits $v_1, \dots, v_{174}$.
  • 83 Check Nodes (CNs): Representing the parity constraint equations $c_1, \dots, c_{83}$.
  • An edge connects VN $v_j$ to CN $c_i$ if and only if $H_{i, j} = 1$.

Soft Demodulation: Bit Log-Likelihood Ratios (LLRs)

Before running the decoder, the demodulator converts the 8 tone energies $E(k, m)$ into soft bit probabilities. Each 8-MFSK tone corresponds to a 3-bit Gray-coded symbol $(b_2, b_1, b_0)$.

The channel prior Log-Likelihood Ratio (LLR) for bit $i \in \{0, 1, 2\}$ of symbol $k$ is:

$$L(b_i) = \ln \left( \frac{P(b_i = 0 \mid \mathbf{Y})}{P(b_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{Y})} \right) = \ln \left( \frac{\sum_{m: b_i(m) = 0} P(E_m)}{\sum_{m: b_i(m) = 1} P(E_m)} \right)$$

Under the Max-Log approximation (standard in SIMD DSP implementations to avoid exponentials), this simplifies to the difference between maximum energies:

$$L(b_i) \approx \gamma \cdot \left( \max_{m: b_i(m) = 0} E_m - \max_{m: b_i(m) = 1} E_m \right)$$

where $\gamma$ is a channel reliability scaling factor proportional to the estimated $E_s / N_0$.

Belief Propagation: The Sum-Product Algorithm

The decoder operates iteratively, passing messages (beliefs) back and forth along the edges of the Tanner graph:

  1. Initialization: Each variable node $v$ is initialized with its channel prior LLR: $L_v^{(0)} = L(b_v)$. Messages from variable nodes to check nodes are initialized: $q_{v \to c} = L_v^{(0)}$.
  2. Check Node Update (CN $\to$ VN): Each check node $c$ aggregates extrinsic beliefs from all connected variable nodes except $v$, enforcing parity: $$\tanh\left(\frac{r_{c \to v}}{2}\right) = \prod_{v' \in \mathcal{N}(c) \setminus \{v\}} \tanh\left(\frac{q_{v' \to c}}{2}\right)$$
  3. Variable Node Update (VN $\to$ CN): Each variable node sums the incoming parity beliefs with its intrinsic channel prior: $$q_{v \to c} = L_v^{(0)} + \sum_{c' \in \mathcal{N}(v) \setminus \{c\}} r_{c' \to v}$$
  4. Syndrome Check and Early Termination: Compute the posterior marginal LLR for each bit: $$\Lambda_v = L_v^{(0)} + \sum_{c \in \mathcal{N}(v)} r_{c \to v}$$ Make a hard bit decision: $\hat{c}_v = 0$ if $\Lambda_v \ge 0$, else $\hat{c}_v = 1$. Evaluate the syndrome vector: $$\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{H} \mathbf{\hat{c}}^T \pmod 2$$ If $\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{0}$, a valid codeword has been discovered! The decoder verifies the 14-bit CRC. If valid, decoding terminates successfully—often in fewer than 15 iterations.
The Min-Sum SIMD Optimization

In production C, Zig, and Rust SDR decoders, computing $\tanh$ and $\operatorname{artanh}$ across thousands of graph edges destroys CPU throughput. Modern decoders employ the Normalized Min-Sum algorithm:

$$r_{c \to v} \approx \alpha \cdot \left( \prod_{v' \in \mathcal{N}(c) \setminus \{v\}} \operatorname{sgn}(q_{v' \to c}) \right) \cdot \min_{v' \in \mathcal{N}(c) \setminus \{v\}} |q_{v' \to c}|$$

with normalization factor $\alpha \approx 0.75 - 0.85$. Because this replaces transcendental math with sign multiplication and finding the minimum absolute integer, it can be vectorized using AVX2 (_mm256_min_epi16) or ARM NEON (vminq_s16), decoding an entire FT8 frame in under $200\ \mu\text{s}$ on a modern CPU core!
