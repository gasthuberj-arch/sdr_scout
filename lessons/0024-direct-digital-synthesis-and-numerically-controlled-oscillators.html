Lesson 0024 Synthesizers & NCOs Reading time: 18 minutes

Direct Digital Synthesis (DDS) & Numerically Controlled Oscillators (NCO): Phase Accumulators, LUT Quantization, and CORDIC

In software-defined radio, generating and manipulating sinusoidal carriers is ubiquitous. Whether translating an RF passband down to complex baseband (Digital Down-Conversion, DDC), tracking carrier frequency offset in a Costas loop (as explored in Lesson 16), steering beams in a phased array (Lesson 20), or synthesizing agile FSK tones for APRS (Lesson 23) and FT8 (Lesson 18), SDR engines must generate continuous-frequency sinusoids with micro-Hertz precision, zero settling time, and pristine spectral purity.

In high-level languages, developers often write :math.cos(theta) or sinf(phase) (as seen in SdrScout.Synth.APRS). While acceptable for offline packet generation at 48 kHz, computing transcendental functions per sample at line rates of 20 to 100+ MSPS cripples memory pipelines and exhausts CPU cycles.

This lesson demystifies the two foundational architectures powering digital frequency synthesis in hardware and high-performance software: Direct Digital Synthesis (DDS) / Numerically Controlled Oscillators (NCO) using truncated phase accumulators with look-up tables (LUTs), and COordinate Rotation DIgital Computation (CORDIC) for multiplierless vector rotation.

1. The Fundamental SDR Problem: The Demise of Analog LOs

Traditional superheterodyne transceivers rely on analog Local Oscillators (LOs) constructed from LC tank circuits, varactor diodes, and analog Phase-Locked Loops (PLLs). While mature, analog synthesizers impose severe physical constraints on software-defined architectures:

  • Settling Latency & Phase Transients: Analog PLLs require hundreds of microseconds or milliseconds to change frequency due to loop filter charge dynamics. Switching frequencies introduces chaotic phase transients, ruling out zero-overhead fast frequency hopping (FHSS) or agile radar chirps.
  • Phase Noise & Thermal Drift: Analog LC resonators suffer from mechanical microphonics, component aging, and thermal frequency drift, requiring constant calibration against temperature-compensated crystal oscillators (TCXOs).
  • Coherence Bottlenecks in Phased Arrays: Synchronizing $M$ distinct analog local oscillators with absolute, deterministic phase alignment across power cycles is an analog calibration nightmare.

A purely digital frequency synthesizer—termed a Numerically Controlled Oscillator (NCO) in software/DSP and a Direct Digital Synthesizer (DDS) when coupled directly to a digital-to-analog converter (DAC)—solves every one of these problems:

  1. Instantaneous Frequency Switching: Changing frequency is achieved by writing a single integer register in a single clock cycle, maintaining seamless phase continuity ($\mathcal{C}^0$).
  2. Micro-Hertz Tuning Resolution: Frequency resolution is governed strictly by accumulator bit-width, achieving micro-Hertz precision across hundreds of megahertz.
  3. Exact Mathematical Phase Coherence: Multiple NCO instances clocked by the same master clock share identical, deterministic phase states, enabling effortless beamforming and MIMO channel alignment.

2. DDS / NCO Architecture: The Core Engine

The classic DDS architecture consists of four sequential stages:

  1. The $N$-bit Phase Accumulator: A digital register that increments by a fixed Frequency Tuning Word ($FTW$) on every clock edge of $f_{\text{clk}}$.
  2. Phase Truncation: Extracting the $W$ most significant bits ($W \ll N$) of the accumulator to address memory.
  3. Phase-to-Amplitude Converter (Sine/Cosine LUT): A memory table mapping discrete phase angles into quantized amplitude samples.
  4. Mixing / Conversion Stage: In a transmitter, feeding an analog DAC with a low-pass reconstruction filter; in a digital receiver (DDC), performing a complex multiplication with incoming $I/Q$ baseband data.

Two's Complement Modular Arithmetic as Circle Traversal

The genius of the phase accumulator lies in binary modulo arithmetic. An $N$-bit unsigned integer register naturally wraps around modulo $2^N$:

$$\theta[n] = (\theta[n-1] + FTW) \pmod{2^N}$$

This digital overflow directly mirrors the geometric topology of the unit circle: traversing an angle from $2\pi - \epsilon$ to $0$ radians is identical to an unsigned accumulator wrapping from $2^N - 1$ back to $0$. There are no conditional branches, no bounds checks, and no transcendental calls. An ordinary hardware adder executes this modulo $2^N$ phase wrapping in a single machine instruction.

3. Frequency Derivation & Micro-Hertz Resolution

Let's derive the exact mathematical relationship governing the synthesized output frequency $f_{\text{out}}$.

The phase accumulator maps the continuous interval $[0, 2\pi)$ onto $2^N$ discrete digital phase states. Each increment of $FTW$ advances the phase by:

$$\Delta\theta = FTW \cdot \frac{2\pi}{2^N}\text{ radians}$$

Since the accumulator updates once per master clock period $T_{\text{clk}} = 1/f_{\text{clk}}$, the discrete-time phase velocity (angular frequency $\omega_{\text{out}}$) is:

$$\omega_{\text{out}} = \frac{\Delta\theta}{T_{\text{clk}}} = \Delta\theta \cdot f_{\text{clk}} = \frac{2\pi \cdot FTW \cdot f_{\text{clk}}}{2^N}\text{ rad/s}$$

Equating $\omega_{\text{out}} = 2\pi f_{\text{out}}$ yields the foundational DDS Frequency Equation:

$$f_{\text{out}} = \frac{f_{\text{clk}} \cdot FTW}{2^N}$$

Deriving Minimum Frequency Step Resolution

The finest frequency adjustment possible corresponds to incrementing the tuning word by the least significant bit ($FTW = 1$):

$$\Delta f_{\text{min}} = \frac{f_{\text{clk}}}{2^N}$$

Notice that the tuning resolution is decoupled from analog components; it depends entirely on the accumulator register width $N$:

  • 32-bit Accumulator at $f_{\text{clk}} = 100\text{ MHz}$ (Standard SDR DDC): $$\Delta f_{\text{min}} = \frac{100 \times 10^6\text{ Hz}}{2^{32}} = \frac{100 \times 10^6}{4,294,967,296} \approx \mathbf{0.02328\text{ Hz}} \quad (23.3\text{ mHz})$$ At 100 MHz, a 32-bit register resolves carrier frequency steps of less than a quarter of a tenth of a Hertz!
  • 48-bit Accumulator at $f_{\text{clk}} = 100\text{ MHz}$ (e.g., AD9910 / AD9954 DDS chips): $$\Delta f_{\text{min}} = \frac{100 \times 10^6\text{ Hz}}{2^{48}} = \frac{100 \times 10^6}{281,474,976,710,656} \approx \mathbf{3.55 \times 10^{-7}\text{ Hz}} = \mathbf{0.355\,\mu\text{Hz}}$$ Sub-micro-Hertz precision allows an SDR transmitter to track satellite Doppler drift or earth-moon-earth (EME) lunar orbital velocity with zero measurable quantization drift over months.
Nyquist Limits & Negative Frequencies

In a real-valued DDS driving an analog DAC, the maximum synthesized frequency cannot exceed the Nyquist boundary $f_{\text{out}} < f_{\text{clk}} / 2$, which corresponds to $FTW < 2^{N-1}$. In practice, anti-aliasing reconstruction filter roll-off limits practical analog DDS outputs to approximately $0.4 f_{\text{clk}}$.

In a complex baseband NCO (mixing complex $I/Q$ signals), the phase accumulator represents analytic rotation $e^{j \omega n}$. Here, $FTW$ can be a signed two's complement integer spanning $[-2^{N-1}, 2^{N-1}-1]$, directly synthesizing negative and positive frequency shifts across $[-f_s/2, +f_s/2]$ without analog aliasing ambiguity.

4. Quantization Dynamics: Phase Truncation vs. Amplitude Quantization

Why can we not simply use the full $N$-bit accumulator to look up sine values?

Consider a 32-bit accumulator. An unconstrained look-up table would require $2^{32}$ entries. If each table entry stores a 16-bit word (2 bytes), the required memory is:

$$2^{32} \times 2\text{ bytes} = 4,294,967,296 \times 2\text{ B} = \mathbf{8.589\text{ Gigabytes}}$$

Storing an 8.5 GB table inside an FPGA block RAM (which tops out at a few tens of megabits) or in high-speed CPU L1 cache (32–64 KB) is physically impossible. Therefore, the system designer must truncate the phase, preserving only the $W$ most significant bits (typically $W = 10$ to $16$) to address the LUT, discarding the lower $B_{\text{trunc}} = N - W$ bits.

Phase Truncation Noise: The Origin of Discrete Spurs

Discarding the lower $(N - W)$ bits introduces a deterministic phase error $\epsilon_\theta[n]$:

$$\theta_{\text{LUT}}[n] = \theta[n] - \epsilon_\theta[n], \quad 0 \le \epsilon_\theta[n] < 2^{N-W}$$

When scaled to radians, $\epsilon_\theta[n] \in [0, 2\pi \cdot 2^{-W})$. The synthesized waveform becomes:

$$s[n] = \cos(\omega_0 n - \epsilon_\theta[n]) \approx \cos(\omega_0 n) + \epsilon_\theta[n] \sin(\omega_0 n)$$

Because $\epsilon_\theta[n]$ is generated by a deterministic accumulator overflow, it is a periodic sawtooth waveform in the time domain. Multiplying the carrier $\sin(\omega_0 n)$ by a periodic error $\epsilon_\theta[n]$ phase-modulates the carrier, producing distinct, sharp spectral spikes: Phase Truncation Spurs.

The Spurious-Free Dynamic Range (SFDR) due to phase truncation is bounded by the famous Nicholas & Samueli Rule:

$$\text{SFDR}_{\text{phase truncation}} \approx 6.02 \cdot W\text{ dBc}$$
  • $W = 10\text{ bits} \implies \text{SFDR} \approx 60.2\text{ dBc}$ (Table size: 1024 words $= 2\text{ KB}$)
  • $W = 12\text{ bits} \implies \text{SFDR} \approx 72.2\text{ dBc}$ (Table size: 4096 words $= 8\text{ KB}$, fits comfortably in L1)
  • $W = 14\text{ bits} \implies \text{SFDR} \approx 84.3\text{ dBc}$ (Table size: 16384 words $= 32\text{ KB}$)
  • $W = 16\text{ bits} \implies \text{SFDR} \approx 96.3\text{ dBc}$ (Table size: 65536 words $= 128\text{ KB}$)

Amplitude Quantization Noise

In addition to phase truncation, the amplitude values stored in the table are quantized to $D$ bits (e.g., $D = 12, 14, 16$). As derived in Lesson 21, uniform amplitude quantization error has variance $\sigma_q^2 = \Delta^2 / 12$, producing a broadband white noise floor:

$$\text{SQNR} \approx 6.02 D + 1.76\text{ dB}$$
The Balanced Design Rule

There is no architectural benefit in making the amplitude word width $D$ significantly larger than the phase address width $W$. If $D = 16$ but $W = 10$, the output will possess a theoretical $98\text{ dB}$ SNR broadband floor, but will be plagued by massive discrete phase spurs at $-60\text{ dBc}$.

Optimal DDS design adheres to the rule: $W \approx D + 2$ to $D + 3$. This guarantees that phase truncation spurs are suppressed beneath the amplitude quantization noise floor.

5. Spurious Reduction Techniques

1. Phase Dithering via PRNG / LFSR

In narrow-band amateur radio receivers, discrete spurs are fatal: if an LO phase spur lands on top of a weak CW or FT8 signal ($-120\text{ dBm}$), it creates a permanent phantom carrier. A uniform white noise floor, however, can be easily integrated out via coherent DSP.

Phase Dithering breaks the periodicity of the phase truncation error $\epsilon_\theta[n]$ by adding a small pseudo-random noise sequence before truncation:

$$\theta_{\text{dithered}}[n] = \theta[n] + \text{LFSR}[n]$$ 
Phase Accumulator [N bits] ----> [ + ] ----> [ Truncate to W bits ] ----> LUT
                                    ^
                                    |
                        [ (N - W)-bit LFSR / PRNG ]

By injecting pseudo-random dither with an amplitude of a few LSBs of the truncated word, the deterministic phase error is modulated into a random, uncorrelated process. The sharp, line-spectrum spurs collapse into a flat, benign Gaussian/uniform noise floor.

Engineering Trade-off: Dithering improves SFDR by $12\text{ dB}$ to $18\text{ dB}$, pushing spurs down toward the amplitude quantization floor at the expense of a negligible $1 - 2\text{ dB}$ increase in the total broadband noise floor.

2. Taylor Series Polynomial Interpolation

Instead of adding noise, we can algebraically approximate the discarded phase bits using Taylor polynomial expansion. Let the accumulator phase $\theta$ be split into a coarse angle $\theta_c$ (the $W$ MSBs) and a fine fractional angle $\theta_f$ (the $N-W$ LSBs):

$$\theta = \theta_c + \theta_f, \quad \text{where } \theta_f \ll 1\text{ rad}$$

Applying the standard trigonometric sum formula:

$$\sin(\theta_c + \theta_f) = \sin(\theta_c)\cos(\theta_f) + \cos(\theta_c)\sin(\theta_f)$$

Since $\theta_f$ is very small, we use first-order Taylor approximations $\cos(\theta_f) \approx 1$ and $\sin(\theta_f) \approx \theta_f$:

$$\sin(\theta_c + \theta_f) \approx \sin(\theta_c) + \theta_f \cdot \cos(\theta_c)$$

With a single hardware Multiply-Accumulate (MAC) unit, the DDS computes:

$$y[n] = \text{LUT}_{\sin}[\theta_c] + \theta_f \cdot \text{LUT}_{\cos}[\theta_c]$$

This technique achieves the spur performance of a 16-bit to 20-bit table ($> 110\text{ dBc}$ SFDR) while using only a compact 10-bit look-up table ($1\text{ KB}$), dramatically reducing memory footprint.

6. The CORDIC Algorithm: Multiplierless Vector Rotation

While LUT-based DDS is fast, look-up tables face severe limitations:

  • They consume valuable on-chip RAM (FPGA Block RAM or CPU L1/L2 cache lines).
  • Memory lookups in SIMD vector engines require vector gathers (e.g., AVX2 _mm256_i32gather_ps), which introduce high latency and memory port contention.
  • In digital down-converters, generating $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ is only half the battle; the samples must then be multiplied by $I_{\text{in}}$ and $Q_{\text{in}}$ using four real hardware multipliers.

In 1959, Jack E. Volder invented CORDIC (COordinate Rotation DIgital Computation). CORDIC generates sine and cosine—and simultaneously performs complex mixing—using only arithmetic bit-shifts and additions.

Mathematical Derivation of CORDIC Rotation

To rotate an arbitrary Cartesian 2D vector $\mathbf{v}_i = [x_i, y_i]^T$ by an angle $\alpha_i$, we apply the standard rotation matrix:

$$\begin{bmatrix} x_{i+1} \\ y_{i+1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\alpha_i & -\sin\alpha_i \\ \sin\alpha_i & \cos\alpha_i \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_i \\ y_i \end{bmatrix}$$

Factoring out $\cos\alpha_i$:

$$\begin{bmatrix} x_{i+1} \\ y_{i+1} \end{bmatrix} = \cos\alpha_i \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -\tan\alpha_i \\ \tan\alpha_i & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_i \\ y_i \end{bmatrix}$$

Volder's Critical Insight: Constrain the elementary rotation steps $\alpha_i$ such that the tangent is an exact negative power of two:

$$\tan\alpha_i = 2^{-i} \quad \implies \quad \alpha_i = \arctan(2^{-i})$$

With this constraint, multiplying by $\tan\alpha_i$ is nothing more than an arithmetic right bit-shift by $i$ bits ($x \gg i$)!

Defining rotation direction $d_i \in \{-1, +1\}$, the pseudo-rotation equations become:

$$x_{i+1} = x_i - d_i \cdot (y_i \gg i)$$ $$y_{i+1} = y_i + d_i \cdot (x_i \gg i)$$ $$z_{i+1} = z_i - d_i \cdot \alpha_i$$

where $z_i$ is the residual angle accumulator. If $z_i \ge 0$, we rotate counter-clockwise ($d_i = +1$); if $z_i < 0$, we rotate clockwise ($d_i = -1$).

The CORDIC Scale Factor $K$

Notice that at each stage, we dropped the cosine pre-factor $\cos\alpha_i$. Therefore, each pseudo-rotation expands the vector magnitude by:

$$\frac{1}{\cos\alpha_i} = \sqrt{1 + \tan^2\alpha_i} = \sqrt{1 + 2^{-2i}}$$

After $M$ iterations, the cumulative scaling gain $A_M$ converges to a fixed mathematical constant:

$$A = \lim_{M \to \infty} \prod_{i=0}^{M-1} \sqrt{1 + 2^{-2i}} \approx 1.646760258121...$$

To obtain true unit-magnitude sine and cosine outputs, we simply pre-scale the initial vector by the reciprocal constant $K = 1/A$:

$$K = \frac{1}{A} \approx \mathbf{0.60725293500888...}$$

Setting initial state:

$$\begin{bmatrix} x_0 \\ y_0 \\ z_0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} K \\ 0 \\ \theta \end{bmatrix} \quad \implies \quad \lim_{M \to \infty} \begin{bmatrix} x_M \\ y_M \\ z_M \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta \\ \sin\theta \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Each CORDIC iteration adds exactly 1 bit of angular precision. Executing 16 iterations yields 16 bits of precision ($> 96\text{ dB}$ dynamic range) with zero multipliers!

Why CORDIC Excels in FPGAs

In an FPGA (such as the AMD Xilinx Artix-7 on a USRP or LimeSDR), dedicated hardware multipliers (DSP48E1 slices) are scarce resources needed for FIR decimation filterbanks and polyphase channelizers. CORDIC unrolls into a deep pipeline of pure logic slices (CLBs, adders, and flip-flops). At every clock cycle, a new sample enters stage 0, and a fully rotated $I/Q$ sample exits stage 16. The engine sustains 1 complex sample per clock cycle at 300+ MHz without utilizing a single DSP block or Block RAM tile.

8. High-Performance C / Rust Implementation

Below is a cache-conscious, production-grade C implementation of an integer NCO with phase truncation, LFSR dithering, and a fixed-point CORDIC vector rotation kernel: 

#include <stdint.h>
#include <math.h>

#define CORDIC_STAGES 16

// Pre-scaled constant K = 0.6072529350 in Q15 format (0.6072529350 * 32768 = 19898)
#define CORDIC_K_Q15 19898

// Precomputed arctan(2^-i) angles scaled to full 32-bit circle (2^32 = 2*pi)
static const uint32_t CORDIC_ANGLES[CORDIC_STAGES] = {
    0x20000000, // atan(1)   = pi/4  (45.000 deg)
    0x12E4051D, // atan(1/2) = 0.463 rad (26.565 deg)
    0x09FB385B, // atan(1/4) = 0.244 rad (14.036 deg)
    0x051111D4, // atan(1/8) = 0.124 rad ( 7.125 deg)
    0x028B0D43, // atan(1/16)= 0.062 rad ( 3.576 deg)
    0x0145D7E1, // atan(1/32)
    0x00A2F61E, // atan(1/64)
    0x00517C55, // atan(1/128)
    0x0028BE53, // atan(1/256)
    0x00145F2E, // atan(1/512)
    0x000A2F98, // atan(1/1024)
    0x000517CC, // atan(1/2048)
    0x00028BE6, // atan(1/4096)
    0x000145F3, // atan(1/8192)
    0x0000A2FA, // atan(1/16384)
    0x0000517D  // atan(1/32768)
};

// 32-bit Galois Linear Feedback Shift Register (LFSR) for Phase Dithering
static inline uint32_t step_lfsr(uint32_t *state) {
    uint32_t lsb = *state & 1;
    *state >>= 1;
    if (lsb) {
        *state ^= 0xD0000001u; // 32-bit maximal polynomial
    }
    return *state;
}

// CORDIC Direct Complex Mixer:
// Rotates incoming (I_in, Q_in) by angle phase_acc using only shifts and adds!
void cordic_rotate_sample(int16_t i_in, int16_t q_in, uint32_t phase,
                          int16_t *i_out, int16_t *q_out) {
    // Map full 32-bit circle into [-pi/2, +pi/2] first quadrant rotation
    int32_t x, y;
    uint32_t z;

    if (phase >= 0x40000000 && phase < 0xC0000000) {
        // Quadrants 2 and 3: rotate by 180 degrees first
        x = -((int32_t)i_in);
        y = -((int32_t)q_in);
        z = phase - 0x80000000;
    } else {
        x = (int32_t)i_in;
        y = (int32_t)q_in;
        z = phase;
    }

    // Pre-scale initial vector by CORDIC gain K (in Q15)
    x = (x * CORDIC_K_Q15) >> 15;
    y = (y * CORDIC_K_Q15) >> 15;

    // Execute 16 shift-and-add stages
    for (int i = 0; i < CORDIC_STAGES; ++i) {
        int32_t x_shift = x >> i;
        int32_t y_shift = y >> i;
        uint32_t target_angle = CORDIC_ANGLES[i];

        if ((int32_t)z >= 0) {
            x -= y_shift;
            y += x_shift;
            z -= target_angle;
        } else {
            x += y_shift;
            y -= x_shift;
            z += target_angle;
        }
    }

    *i_out = (int16_t)x;
    *q_out = (int16_t)y;
}

Checking Your Mastery

1. A digital down-converter (DDC) employs a 32-bit phase accumulator clocked at $f_{\text{clk}} = 125\text{ MHz}$. What is the frequency step resolution $\Delta f_{\text{min}}$, and how does the accumulator maintain continuous phase when frequency is shifted instantaneously?
  • $\Delta f \approx 0.029\text{ Hz}$; unsigned integer modular overflow automatically preserves phase continuity across tuning word updates.
  • $\Delta f \approx 0.058\text{ Hz}$; a software interrupt service routine resets the accumulator phase to zero upon tuning word changes.
  • $\Delta f \approx 0.015\text{ Hz}$; a fractional phase-locked loop filter smooths the frequency transition over several clock periods.
  • $\Delta f \approx 0.073\text{ Hz}$; hardware floating-point registers interpolate the phase trajectory via cubic spline smoothing.
Correct! The frequency resolution is $\Delta f_{\text{min}} = \frac{f_{\text{clk}}}{2^N} = \frac{125 \times 10^6}{2^{32}} \approx 0.0291\text{ Hz}$. Because the phase state $\theta[n]$ is maintained in the register and only the step size $FTW$ changes on the next clock edge, $\theta[n+1] = \theta[n] + FTW_{\text{new}} \pmod{2^N}$. The phase trajectory remains continuous ($\mathcal{C}^0$) with zero transient settling time.
2. In a DDS architecture with a 32-bit phase accumulator and a 12-bit LUT address ($W = 12$) storing 14-bit amplitude samples ($D = 14$), what is the dominant spectral degradation, and how does LFSR phase dithering eliminate it?
  • Periodic phase truncation creates discrete spectral spurs around $-72\text{ dBc}$; adding dither decorrelates truncation error into benign white noise.
  • Amplitude quantization noise generates a flat noise floor at $-86\text{ dBc}$; adding dither cancels the quantization error via negative feedback.
  • Accumulator integer overflow injects harmonic distortion at $-48\text{ dBc}$; adding dither limits the phase range to the first Nyquist zone.
  • Lookup table address aliasing introduces intermodulation distortion at $-60\text{ dBc}$; adding dither dynamically expands the table dimensions.
Correct! Truncating the phase from 32 bits to $W = 12$ bits produces a periodic sawtooth phase error that acts as phase modulation, creating discrete spurs bounded by $\approx 6.02 \cdot W = 72.2\text{ dBc}$. In contrast, 14-bit amplitude quantization creates a flat broadband noise floor at $6.02(14) + 1.76 \approx 86\text{ dB}$. Adding pseudo-random LFSR dither randomizes the phase error periodicity, converting discrete spurs into flat white noise and boosting SFDR by $12\text{--}18\text{ dB}$.
3. Why does the CORDIC vector rotation algorithm outperform memory lookup tables (LUTs) in high-throughput FPGA DSP slices and wide SIMD vector registers?
  • It relies strictly on bit-shifts and additions, avoiding memory bandwidth bottlenecks, cache misses, and dedicated hardware multiplier consumption.
  • It computes full floating-point transcendental trigonometric functions directly within hardware logic slices in a single clock cycle.
  • It completely eliminates amplitude scaling error without requiring initial vector pre-scaling or post-rotation normalization stages.
  • It compresses large trigonometric lookup tables into specialized L1 instruction cache lines using lossless entropy coding techniques.
Correct! In FPGAs, CORDIC maps entirely to distributed logic slices (LUTs and adders), saving scarce hardware multipliers (DSP slices) and Block RAM. In SIMD CPU registers, CORDIC eliminates non-contiguous table gather loads and cache misses, operating entirely within vector ALU registers via pipelined arithmetic bit-shifts.

Summary & Architecture Implications

Direct Digital Synthesis and Numerically Controlled Oscillators represent the bridge between pure discrete mathematics and real-world RF signals:

  1. The Phase Accumulator: Natural $N$-bit modular arithmetic converts a simple integer addition into continuous traversal of the unit circle, offering micro-Hertz resolution ($\Delta f = f_{\text{clk}} / 2^N$) and instantaneous phase-continuous tuning.
  2. The Truncation Trade-off: Memory constraints mandate truncating the phase to $W$ bits. This periodic truncation generates discrete phase spurs bounded by $\text{SFDR} \approx 6.02 W\text{ dBc}$.
  3. Spur Remediation: LFSR phase dithering smears discrete spurs into benign white noise floor ($+12 - 18\text{ dB}$ SFDR gain), while Taylor interpolation achieves $>110\text{ dBc}$ SFDR using a compact 10-bit table and a single MAC unit.
  4. CORDIC Supremacy: By constraining rotation angles to $\arctan(2^{-i})$, CORDIC eliminates memory lookups and multipliers entirely, rotating complex baseband vectors via pure bit-shifts and additions.
