Timing Error Detectors & Symbol Clock Synchronization: Gardner, Mueller & Müller, and Farrow Interpolators

In Lesson 16, we analyzed carrier frequency offset (CFO) and derived the Costas loop for phase tracking in coherent digital modes. However, our Costas loop operated under an implicit prerequisite: that the receiver already possessed clean, symbol-rate complex samples taken at the exact peak of the open eye diagram.

In an asynchronous Software-Defined Radio, this prerequisite is physically impossible without dedicated symbol timing recovery. The Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) clock runs completely unsynchronized from the remote transmitter's symbol clock. The ratio of sample rate to symbol rate is generally irrational, subject to thermal crystal drift, and the optimal strobe instant almost never falls on an integer ADC sample index.

This lesson bridges the continuous physics of RF transmission and discrete-time DSP architecture. We will analyze the asynchronous sampling problem, rigorously derive the non-data-aided Gardner Timing Error Detector (TED) and prove why it is mathematically independent of carrier phase, examine the low-overhead decision-directed Mueller & Müller TED, analyze closed-loop stability using S-curves and phase detector gain $K_d$, and construct a high-throughput, SIMD-friendly Farrow polynomial interpolator driven by a decremented modulo-1 controller.

1. The Asynchronous Sampling Dilemma

Consider a transmitter generating a digital baseband stream at symbol rate $R_{\text{sym}} = 1 / T_{\text{sym}}$ baud. The transmitted continuous-time signal is:

$$s(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} a_k \, p(t - k T_{\text{sym}} - \tau_0)$$

where $a_k \in \mathbb{C}$ are modulation symbols, $p(t)$ is the transmit pulse shape (such as a Root Raised Cosine filter), and $\tau_0 \in [0, T_{\text{sym}})$ is an arbitrary channel propagation delay.

At the receiver, the front-end ADC samples the continuous baseband signal at a fixed sampling frequency $f_s = 1 / T_s$. Crucially:

  1. Independent Physical Clocks: The transmitter DAC clock and the receiver ADC clock are generated by separate quartz crystals. Their frequency ratio is not an exact integer: $$Q_{\text{nom}} = \frac{T_{\text{sym}}}{T_s} = \frac{f_s}{R_{\text{sym}}} \notin \mathbb{Z}$$ For example, sampling a $1.25\text{ MBaud}$ digital mode with an RTL-SDR at $f_s = 2.4\text{ MSPS}$ yields $Q = 1.92$ samples per symbol.
  2. Clock Drift ($\Delta T / T$): Even if $Q_{\text{nom}}$ were chosen as an integer, crystal manufacturing tolerances and thermal fluctuations ($\pm 10\text{ to } 50\text{ ppm}$) cause the clocks to slip continuously: $$\frac{\Delta T_{\text{sym}}}{T_{\text{sym}}} = \frac{T_{\text{sym}}^{\text{rx}} - T_{\text{sym}}^{\text{tx}}}{T_{\text{sym}}^{\text{tx}}}$$ At $50\text{ ppm}$ drift, a receiver drifts by an entire symbol period every $20,000$ symbols.
  3. Fractional Sampling Offset: The continuous optimum strobe instant for symbol $k$ is $t_k = k T_{\text{sym}} + \tau_k$. In the discrete ADC sample grid $n T_s$, $t_k$ is decomposed into an integer basepoint sample index $m_k$ and a dimensionless fractional delay $\mu_k \in [0, 1)$: $$t_k = (m_k + \mu_k) T_s, \quad m_k = \left\lfloor \frac{t_k}{T_s} \right\rfloor, \quad \mu_k = \frac{t_k}{T_s} - m_k$$
The Failure of Nearest-Neighbor Slicing

If a receiver simply selects the closest integer ADC sample ($m_k = \text{round}(t_k / T_s)$), the residual timing error $|\mu_k - 0.5| T_s$ can reach up to $\pm 0.5 T_s$.

In systems operating at $Q = 2$ samples/symbol, an error of $0.5 T_s$ equals $0.25 T_{\text{sym}}$. At this offset on a Root Raised Cosine pulse, the eye diagram is partially closed: the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) degrades by several dB, severe Intersymbol Interference (ISI) is injected from adjacent symbols, and periodic sample insertion/deletion causes catastrophic cycle slips.

Therefore, the receiver must perform continuous fractional delay interpolation in software, adjusting both the strobe instant $m_k$ and the fractional sub-sample shift $\mu_k$ dynamically.

2. The Farrow Structure Interpolator

How can software reconstruct the exact continuous value $x((m + \mu)T_s)$ from discrete samples $x[n] = x(n T_s)$ when $\mu \in [0, 1)$ changes on every single symbol?

The mathematically ideal interpolator is the Shannon-Whittaker sinc formula:

$$x(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n] \operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{t - n T_s}{T_s}\right)$$

Evaluating an infinite, non-causal sinc series in real-time streaming DSP is impossible. Truncating the sinc requires dynamic filter tap recalculation: for every new $\mu$, a new set of FIR filter taps $h_k(\mu) = \operatorname{sinc}(k - \mu)$ must be evaluated via transcendental functions. This stalls CPU pipelines and ruins cache locality.

Cecil W. Farrow's Architectural Breakthrough

In 1988, Cecil W. Farrow published a landmark architecture: approximate the continuous signal over a local window of $2N$ samples using an $P$-th order polynomial in $\mu$:

$$y[m + \mu] = \sum_{l=0}^{P} c_l[m] \, \mu^l$$

Farrow realized that each polynomial coefficient $c_l[m]$ can be expressed as the output of a fixed-coefficient discrete FIR filter convolved with the input sample sequence $x[n]$:

$$c_l[m] = \sum_{k=-N+1}^{N} C_{l, k} \, x[m - k]$$

This decouples the interpolation into two orthogonal, highly optimizable operations:

  1. Static FIR Filter Banks: The filter tap matrices $C_{l, k}$ are time-invariant mathematical constants. They do not depend on $\mu$! In modern SIMD architectures (AVX2, AVX-512, ARM NEON, or Zig @Vector), these static convolutions run at peak hardware multiply-accumulate (MAC) line rate.
  2. Horner's Rule Polynomial Evaluation: Once the intermediate vector values $v_l[m] = c_l[m]$ are computed, the interpolated sample is evaluated with minimal operations using nested multiplications: $$y[m + \mu] = v_0[m] + \mu \Big( v_1[m] + \mu \big( v_2[m] + \mu \, v_3[m] \big) \Big)$$

4-Tap Cubic Hermite Polynomials

While classic Lagrange interpolation passes exactly through grid points, it exhibits slope discontinuities at sample boundaries. In software radio, a 4-tap piecewise cubic Hermite interpolator ($P=3$, $2N=4$, using samples $x[m-1], x[m], x[m+1], x[m+2]$) is strongly preferred because it guarantees continuous first derivatives ($C^1$ continuity):

$$\begin{aligned} v_0[m] &= x[m] \\ v_1[m] &= -\frac{1}{2} x[m-1] + \frac{1}{2} x[m+1] \\ v_2[m] &= x[m-1] - \frac{5}{2} x[m] + 2 x[m+1] - \frac{1}{2} x[m+2] \\ v_3[m] &= -\frac{1}{2} x[m-1] + \frac{3}{2} x[m] - \frac{3}{2} x[m+1] + \frac{1}{2} x[m+2] \end{aligned}$$

Notice that at $\mu = 0$, $y[m] = v_0[m] = x[m]$. At $\mu = 1$, the sum $v_0 + v_1 + v_2 + v_3 = x[m+1]$. The boundary slope matches the central difference gradient $\frac{x[m+1] - x[m-1]}{2}$, eliminating high-frequency phase discontinuities in the interpolator output.

3. The Gardner Timing Error Detector (TED)

Now that we have a fractional interpolator, we need an error metric that tells us whether the current sampling instant is early or late.

In 1986, Floyd M. Gardner introduced a Non-Data-Aided (NDA) algorithm operating at 2 samples per symbol. It requires no prior knowledge of the transmitted data and no training preambles:

  • $y[k]$: The current on-time symbol strobe.
  • $y[k-1]$: The previous on-time symbol strobe.
  • $y[k - 1/2]$: The mid-point transition sample halfway between $y[k-1]$ and $y[k]$.

For a real-valued signal, the Gardner timing error is:

$$e[k] = y\left[k - \frac{1}{2}\right] \cdot \Big( y[k] - y[k-1] \Big)$$

For complex baseband signals $y[n] = I[n] + j Q[n]$, the error is summed across both quadrature rails:

$$e[k] = I\left[k - \frac{1}{2}\right] \Big( I[k] - I[k-1] \Big) + Q\left[k - \frac{1}{2}\right] \Big( Q[k] - Q[k-1] \Big)$$

The Mathematical Proof: Complete Carrier Phase Independence

In an SDR receiver architecture, a fundamental design question arises: Should symbol timing recovery occur before or after carrier frequency/phase recovery?

If the timing error detector requires a coherent carrier, carrier recovery and timing recovery become mutually dependent: neither loop can lock until the other has locked.

The Gardner Invariance Theorem

The Gardner TED is completely invariant to carrier phase offset and carrier frequency offset. Timing recovery can achieve full lock even when the carrier constellation is spinning at thousands of radians per second.

Let us prove this rigorously. Suppose the received complex baseband signal suffers an arbitrary, unknown carrier phase offset $\theta$:

$$r[n] = y[n] \, e^{j\theta} = \big(I[n] + j Q[n]\big)(\cos\theta + j\sin\theta)$$

Separating into real and imaginary parts:

$$\begin{aligned} I_r[n] &= I[n]\cos\theta - Q[n]\sin\theta \\ Q_r[n] &= I[n]\sin\theta + Q[n]\cos\theta \end{aligned}$$

Let $\Delta I = I[k] - I[k-1]$ and $\Delta Q = Q[k] - Q[k-1]$. Because differentiation is linear:

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta I_r &= \Delta I \cos\theta - \Delta Q \sin\theta \\ \Delta Q_r &= \Delta I \sin\theta + \Delta Q \cos\theta \end{aligned}$$

Similarly, denote the mid-symbol samples as $I_m = I[k - 1/2]$ and $Q_m = Q[k - 1/2]$:

$$\begin{aligned} I_{r, m} &= I_m \cos\theta - Q_m \sin\theta \\ Q_{r, m} &= I_m \sin\theta + Q_m \cos\theta \end{aligned}$$

Now evaluate the Gardner error $e_r[k] = I_{r, m} \Delta I_r + Q_{r, m} \Delta Q_r$ on the phase-rotated signal:

$$\begin{aligned} I_{r, m} \Delta I_r &= (I_m \cos\theta - Q_m \sin\theta)(\Delta I \cos\theta - \Delta Q \sin\theta) \\ &= I_m \Delta I \cos^2\theta - (I_m \Delta Q + Q_m \Delta I)\cos\theta\sin\theta + Q_m \Delta Q \sin^2\theta \\[1em] Q_{r, m} \Delta Q_r &= (I_m \sin\theta + Q_m \cos\theta)(\Delta I \sin\theta + \Delta Q \cos\theta) \\ &= I_m \Delta I \sin^2\theta + (I_m \Delta Q + Q_m \Delta I)\cos\theta\sin\theta + Q_m \Delta Q \cos^2\theta \end{aligned}$$

Adding the two terms together:

$$\begin{aligned} e_r[k] &= I_m \Delta I \big(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta\big) + Q_m \Delta Q \big(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta\big) \\ &\quad + \big[-(I_m \Delta Q + Q_m \Delta I) + (I_m \Delta Q + Q_m \Delta I)\big]\cos\theta\sin\theta \\[1em] &= I_m \Delta I \cdot (1) + Q_m \Delta Q \cdot (1) + 0 \\[1em] &= I\left[k - \frac{1}{2}\right]\big(I[k] - I[k-1]\big) + Q\left[k - \frac{1}{2}\right]\big(Q[k] - Q[k-1]\big) \\[1em] &\equiv e[k] \quad \blacksquare \end{aligned}$$

The phase angle $\theta$ cancels out entirely. The cross-terms vanish identically, and the trigonometric terms sum to unity via Pythagoras. As a result, the Gardner TED can be placed directly after the matched filter and prior to the Costas loop in the SDR processing graph.

4. The Mueller & Müller (M&M) Decision-Directed TED

While the Gardner detector is robust and phase-independent, it requires 2 samples per symbol. In an SDR running on an embedded CPU or high-speed FPGA, evaluating the Farrow interpolator twice per symbol doubles the compute load and memory bandwidth.

In 1976, K. H. Mueller and M. Müller introduced a decision-directed algorithm that operates at exactly 1 sample per symbol.

Mathematical Formulation

Let $y[k]$ be the on-time complex sample, and let $\hat{a}[k] = \operatorname{slice}(y[k])$ be the hard decision symbol chosen by the constellation slicer (e.g., $\hat{a}_k = \pm 1 \pm j$ for QPSK).

The complex Mueller & Müller timing error is:

$$e[k] = \operatorname{Re}\left\{ y[k] \, \hat{a}^*[k-1] - y[k-1] \, \hat{a}^*[k] \right\}$$

In Cartesian coordinates, letting $y[k] = I[k] + j Q[k]$ and $\hat{a}[k] = \hat{a}_I[k] + j \hat{a}_Q[k]$:

$$e[k] = \hat{a}_I[k-1] I[k] - \hat{a}_I[k] I[k-1] + \hat{a}_Q[k-1] Q[k] - \hat{a}_Q[k] Q[k-1]$$

Architectural Trade-Off: Gardner vs. Mueller & Müller

Attribute Gardner TED Mueller & Müller TED
Sampling Requirement 2 samples / symbol (on-time + mid-point) 1 sample / symbol (on-time only)
Interpolator Workload $2 \times$ Farrow evaluations per baud $1 \times$ Farrow evaluation per baud (50% reduction)
Carrier Phase Sensitivity Completely Phase-Independent (runs before Costas loop) Highly Sensitive (decisions $\hat{a}[k]$ fail if carrier phase spins)
Decision Dependency Non-Data-Aided (blind, no slicer needed) Decision-Directed (requires slicer decisions)

Because M&M requires reliable slicer decisions $\hat{a}[k]$, it cannot easily acquire timing if a large Carrier Frequency Offset rotates the constellation points past decision boundaries. Hence, receivers often use Gardner for initial synchronization, or run M&M jointly inside the Costas loop de-rotation stage.

5. S-Curve Analysis & Phase Detector Gain $K_d$

A Timing Error Detector is a non-linear stochastic device. At any individual symbol $k$, the error output $e[k]$ fluctuates widely due to both additive thermal noise and data pattern transitions (known as self-noise or pattern jitter).

To evaluate whether the tracking loop will converge, control engineers analyze the S-Curve (detector characteristic curve), defined as the expected value of the error detector output conditioned on a static timing error $\tau$:

$$S(\tau) = \mathbb{E}\big[ e[k] \mid \tau \big]$$

Derivation of the Gardner S-Curve

Assume transmitted symbols $a_k$ are zero-mean, independent, and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with variance $\sigma_a^2 = \mathbb{E}[a_k^2]$, passed through an overall Nyquist pulse shape $g(t) = p(t) * p^*(-t)$ (such as a raised cosine pulse).

The received samples at timing offset $\tau$ are:

$$y[k] = \sum_n a_n g\big((k - n)T_{\text{sym}} + \tau\big)$$

Substituting into the Gardner error formula and taking the expectation $\mathbb{E}[\cdot]$:

$$S(\tau) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \left(\sum_n a_n g\left((k - n - 1/2)T + \tau\right)\right) \left(\sum_m a_m \big[ g((k - m)T + \tau) - g((k - 1 - m)T + \tau) \big]\right) \right]$$

Because the data symbols are uncorrelated ($\mathbb{E}[a_n a_m] = \sigma_a^2 \delta_{n, m}$), the double sum collapses to a single sum over $m$:

$$S(\tau) = \sigma_a^2 \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} g\left(m T + \frac{T}{2} + \tau\right) \Big[ g(m T + \tau) - g((m + 1)T + \tau) \Big]$$

For typical pulses with energy concentrated near the origin, the dominant terms occur at $m = 0$ and $m = -1$:

$$S(\tau) \approx \sigma_a^2 \, g(\tau) \left[ g\left(\tau - \frac{T_{\text{sym}}}{2}\right) - g\left(\tau + \frac{T_{\text{sym}}}{2}\right) \right]$$

Phase Detector Gain $K_d$

Near zero timing error ($\tau \approx 0$), the S-curve is approximately linear: $S(\tau) \approx K_d \tau$. The slope at the origin defines the detector gain $K_d$:

$$K_d = \left. \frac{d S(\tau)}{d\tau} \right|_{\tau=0} = -2 \, \sigma_a^2 \, g(0) \, \left. \frac{d g(t)}{dt} \right|_{t = T_{\text{sym}} / 2}$$
The Excess Bandwidth ($\alpha$) Dependence

Notice that $K_d$ depends directly on the derivative of the pulse shape evaluated at the half-symbol point $t = T_{\text{sym}} / 2$.

For a Raised Cosine pulse with roll-off factor $\alpha$: $$\left. \frac{d g(t)}{dt} \right|_{t = T_{\text{sym}} / 2} \propto -\frac{\sin(\pi \alpha / 2)}{1 - \alpha^2}$$ If the transmit filter uses a sharp cut-off ($\alpha \to 0$, brickwall Nyquist spectrum), the slope at the half-symbol point approaches zero, driving $K_d \to 0$. In this regime, the Gardner TED produces virtually zero timing error signal and is overwhelmed by pattern jitter!

The Gardner detector operates optimally when excess bandwidth is moderate to high ($\alpha \ge 0.35$).

6. Loop Filter & Modulo-1 Interpolation Controller

To close the timing synchronization loop, the error metric $e[k]$ must drive an accumulator that commands the Farrow interpolator. This is executed using a Decremented Modulo-1 Controller (a discrete Numerically Controlled Oscillator).

Controller Mechanics

Let $T_s$ be the ADC sample period and $T_{\text{sym}}$ be the symbol period. The nominal step size per input sample is the inverse oversampling ratio:

$$W_0 = \frac{T_s}{T_{\text{sym}}} = \frac{1}{Q_{\text{nom}}}$$

The controller maintains an internal phase accumulator $\eta[n] \in [0, 1)$. At every incoming ADC sample $n$, the controller decrements $\eta$ by the control word $W[n]$:

$$\eta_{\text{temp}} = \eta[n-1] - W[n]$$

where $W[n] = W_0 + v[n]$ is dynamically adjusted by a Proportional-Integral (PI) loop filter driven by error $e[k]$:

$$\begin{aligned} v_I[k] &= v_I[k-1] + K_i \, e[k] \quad &\text{(Integral Frequency Tracking)} \\ v[k] &= v_I[k] + K_p \, e[k] \quad &\text{(Proportional Phase Tracking)} \end{aligned}$$

Underflow Detection & Fractional Delay $\mu$

An underflow ($\eta_{\text{temp}} < 0$) signals that the continuous symbol strobe instant $t_k$ occurred between sample $n-1$ and sample $n$!

When underflow occurs:

  1. Compute Fractional Delay $\mu$: By linear interpolation between $\eta[n-1]$ and $\eta_{\text{temp}}$, the exact fractional distance is: $$\mu_k = \frac{\eta[n-1]}{W[n]}$$
  2. Evaluate Interpolator: Run the Farrow interpolator with basepoint sample $x[n]$ and fractional delay $\mu_k$.
  3. Assert Symbol Strobe: Deliver the interpolated sample $y[k]$ to the downstream demodulator / TED.
  4. Modulo-1 Wrap: Add $1.0$ to the accumulator to complete the cyclic wrap: $$\eta[n] = \eta_{\text{temp}} + 1.0$$

Because Gardner requires 2 samples/symbol, the controller decrements by $2 W[n]$ (or updates at twice the baud rate), alternately generating mid-point ($k - 1/2$) and on-time ($k$) strobes.

7. Zero-Allocation C99 Implementation

The following production-grade C implementation demonstrates a complete, streaming symbol timing recovery module. It features 4-tap cubic Hermite Farrow interpolation, a decremented modulo-1 NCO, and phase-independent complex Gardner error detection: 

#include <math.h>
#include <stdbool.h>
#include <stddef.h>

typedef struct {
    float i;
    float q;
} complex_f32;

typedef struct {
    // Controller state
    float eta;           // Modulo-1 phase accumulator [0, 1)
    float w;             // Current step size (T_s / (T_sym / 2))
    float w_nom;         // Nominal step size (2.0f / sps_nom)

    // PI Loop Filter coefficients
    float kp;            // Proportional gain
    float ki;            // Integral gain
    float vi;            // Integrator state (frequency drift)

    // Sample history for 4-tap Farrow FIR
    complex_f32 hist[4];

    // Gardner state registers
    complex_f32 prev_on_time;
    complex_f32 mid_point;
    bool strobe_is_on_time;
} symbol_sync_t;

void symbol_sync_init(symbol_sync_t *s, float sps_nom, float loop_bw, float damping) {
    s->w_nom = 2.0f / sps_nom; // 2 strobes per symbol for Gardner
    s->w = s->w_nom;
    s->eta = 1.0f;
    s->vi = 0.0f;
    s->strobe_is_on_time = false;

    s->prev_on_time = (complex_f32){0.0f, 0.0f};
    s->mid_point    = (complex_f32){0.0f, 0.0f};
    for (int i = 0; i < 4; i++) s->hist[i] = (complex_f32){0.0f, 0.0f};

    // Synthesize PI loop gains from normalized bandwidth and damping
    // Rice, Digital Communications: A Discrete-Time Approach
    float denom = 1.0f + 2.0f * damping * loop_bw + loop_bw * loop_bw;
    s->kp = (4.0f * damping * loop_bw) / denom;
    s->ki = (4.0f * loop_bw * loop_bw) / denom;
}

// 4-Tap Cubic Hermite Farrow Interpolator (Horner's Method)
static inline complex_f32 farrow_interpolate(const complex_f32 hist[4], float mu) {
    // hist[0]=x[m+2], hist[1]=x[m+1], hist[2]=x[m], hist[3]=x[m-1]
    complex_f32 ym1 = hist[3];
    complex_f32 y0  = hist[2];
    complex_f32 y1  = hist[1];
    complex_f32 y2  = hist[0];

    // Evaluate FIR branch filters for I
    float v0_i = y0.i;
    float v1_i = -0.5f * ym1.i + 0.5f * y1.i;
    float v2_i = ym1.i - 2.5f * y0.i + 2.0f * y1.i - 0.5f * y2.i;
    float v3_i = -0.5f * ym1.i + 1.5f * y0.i - 1.5f * y1.i + 0.5f * y2.i;

    // Evaluate FIR branch filters for Q
    float v0_q = y0.q;
    float v1_q = -0.5f * ym1.q + 0.5f * y1.q;
    float v2_q = ym1.q - 2.5f * y0.q + 2.0f * y1.q - 0.5f * y2.q;
    float v3_q = -0.5f * ym1.q + 1.5f * y0.q - 1.5f * y1.q + 0.5f * y2.q;

    // Horner's evaluation: v0 + mu*(v1 + mu*(v2 + mu*v3))
    complex_f32 out;
    out.i = v0_i + mu * (v1_i + mu * (v2_i + mu * v3_i));
    out.q = v0_q + mu * (v1_q + mu * (v2_q + mu * v3_q));
    return out;
}

// Process a contiguous block of ADC samples, producing synchronized symbol-rate output
size_t symbol_sync_process(symbol_sync_t *s,
                           const complex_f32 *in, size_t in_count,
                           complex_f32 *out_symbols) {
    size_t out_count = 0;

    for (size_t n = 0; n < in_count; n++) {
        // Shift sample history pipeline
        s->hist[3] = s->hist[2];
        s->hist[2] = s->hist[1];
        s->hist[1] = s->hist[0];
        s->hist[0] = in[n];

        // Decrement Modulo-1 NCO
        float eta_next = s->eta - s->w;

        if (eta_next < 0.0f) {
            // Underflow event: Strobe instant falls between hist[2] and hist[1]
            float mu = s->eta / s->w; // Fractional delay in [0, 1)

            // Reconstruct continuous sample via Farrow
            complex_f32 sample = farrow_interpolate(s->hist, mu);

            if (s->strobe_is_on_time) {
                // On-time symbol strobe: evaluate Gardner TED
                // e = I_mid*(I_on - I_prev) + Q_mid*(Q_on - Q_prev)
                float dI = sample.i - s->prev_on_time.i;
                float dQ = sample.q - s->prev_on_time.q;
                float err = s->mid_point.i * dI + s->mid_point.q * dQ;

                // Update PI loop filter
                s->vi += s->ki * err;
                // Clamp integrator to +/- 5% nominal step to prevent runaway
                if (s->vi >  0.05f * s->w_nom) s->vi =  0.05f * s->w_nom;
                if (s->vi < -0.05f * s->w_nom) s->vi = -0.05f * s->w_nom;

                s->w = s->w_nom + s->vi + s->kp * err;

                // Store current on-time symbol
                s->prev_on_time = sample;
                out_symbols[out_count++] = sample;
            } else {
                // Mid-point transition strobe: store sample
                s->mid_point = sample;
            }

            s->strobe_is_on_time = !s->strobe_is_on_time;
            s->eta = eta_next + 1.0f; // Modulo-1 wrap
        } else {
            s->eta = eta_next;
        }
    }

    return out_count;
}

Checking Your Mastery

1. Why is the complex Gardner Timing Error Detector mathematically invariant to carrier phase offset, allowing symbol synchronization to precede carrier recovery?
  • The detector applies a pre-slicer Hilbert transform that projects all constellation energy onto the real I-axis.
  • Expanding cross-products of phase-rotated I and Q rails cancels cross-terms, leaving the sum scaled by sin²θ + cos²θ = 1.
  • The mid-point sample is forced to zero during bit transitions, which suppresses carrier rotation in the multipliers.
  • The modulo-1 phase accumulator automatically absorbs carrier angular frequency as an imaginary step component.
Correct! When the complex Gardner error $e_r[k] = I_r[k-1/2]\Delta I_r + Q_r[k-1/2]\Delta Q_r$ is expanded with $I_r = I\cos\theta - Q\sin\theta$ and $Q_r = I\sin\theta + Q\cos\theta$, the cross-terms $(I_m \Delta Q + Q_m \Delta I)\cos\theta\sin\theta$ have opposite signs and cancel completely. The remaining terms group as $(I_m \Delta I + Q_m \Delta Q)(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = e[k]$, making the error completely independent of carrier phase angle $\theta$.
2. In real-time SDR systems, what architectural advantage makes the Farrow polynomial interpolator superior to dynamic sinc or Lagrange tap recalculation?
  • It eliminates all hardware multiplier stages by replacing polynomial operations with fast bitwise barrel shifting.
  • It converts non-causal IIR feedback loops into perfectly linear-phase all-pass recursive digital filters.
  • Its branch FIR filter coefficients are static compile-time constants, isolating the variable delay μ to Horner MACs.
  • It guarantees zero intersymbol interference across all pulse shaping filters without requiring matched filtering.
Correct! In the Farrow structure, the filter coefficients $C_{l, k}$ of the branch FIR filters are fixed mathematical constants. They can be compiled into static SIMD vector instructions (AVX2/NEON) that execute at peak line rate. The continuously varying fractional delay $\mu$ is applied only at the scalar or vector output via Horner's rule, eliminating expensive per-sample tap recalculations.
3. What is the fundamental operational trade-off when selecting a Mueller & Müller TED over a Gardner TED?
  • Mueller & Müller requires four samples per symbol, quadrupling memory bandwidth but widening linear lock range.
  • Mueller & Müller cuts interpolation rate to 1 sample/symbol, but fails if carrier phase is spinning or SNR is low.
  • Mueller & Müller is completely non-data-aided, eliminating pattern jitter at the cost of narrower loop bandwidth.
  • Mueller & Müller operates only on binary FSK signals and cannot track quadrature amplitude modulation schemes.
Correct! Mueller & Müller operates at 1 sample/symbol, reducing Farrow interpolation and filtering operations by 50% relative to Gardner's 2 samples/symbol. However, because it is decision-directed ($e = \text{Re}\{y_k \hat{a}_{k-1}^* - y_{k-1} \hat{a}_k^*\}$), any uncorrected carrier frequency offset that rotates constellation points causes false slicer decisions, destroying the timing gradient.
